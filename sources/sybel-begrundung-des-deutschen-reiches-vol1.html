Sybel — Die Begründung des Deutschen Reiches durch Wilhelm I., Vol. 1

Heinrich von Sybel (1817–1895) — Die Begründung des Deutschen Reiches durch Wilhelm I. Erster Band. — 2. unveränderte Auflage. München und Leipzig: Verlag von R. Oldenbourg, 1889.

The opening volume of Sybel’s multi-volume history of the foundation of the German Empire under Wilhelm I. (“William I”). In the Vorrede (“preface”) Sybel explains that after his Geschichte der Revolutionszeit 1789–1800 had traced the collapse of the Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation, the events of 1866 and 1870 made him wish to recount the rebirth of the German Reich from authentic sources. Political obstacles delayed the plan until Fürst Bismarck (“Prince Bismarck”) allowed publication of his Frankfurt reports and letters and, on 19 March 1881, authorized use of the Prussian state archives and the Foreign Office registry for Prussian history 1850–1870. Sybel inventories the material he was permitted to see: ministerial decrees and ambassadors’ reports, conference protocols, telegrams and correspondence of every kind, notes and despatches of foreign powers, chamber proceedings and newspaper cuttings, all ordered in long series of hundreds of file bundles. Desired supplements came from the records of the State Ministry and of the Great General Staff, from oral communications of persons who took part in or stood near the events, and, for the opposing policy, from the older archives of Hanover, Electoral Hesse, and Nassau. He claims that after countless incomplete, half-true, or untrue accounts, the possibility now existed to write the history of those decades from the documents that grew out of Prussian action or determined it, following every turn of policy day by day and in decisive crises hour by hour.

This first volume runs from the revolutionary crisis of 1848–49 through the Prussian Union project and the Olmützer Punktation (“Punctation of Olmütz”) of late 1850. Sybel’s interpretive line is already firm: German unity under a hereditary empire offered by a popular assembly failed because Prussia’s king would not accept a crown from revolution and because Austria and the secondary states would not accept Prussian hegemony without coercion; the dualist contest ends, for this volume, in Austria’s temporary restoration of the old Bund (“Confederation”). The books that follow inside the volume—National Assembly, imperial question, Union policy, renunciation of the Union—form a single dramatic arc from parliamentary hope to cabinet defeat.

Erstes Buch — Die Nationalversammlung (The National Assembly)

Sybel opens with the Frankfurt Nationalversammlung (“National Assembly”) as the institutional expression of the 1848 national movement. He presents it not as a free creation of the German people alone but as the product of a Europe-wide revolutionary shock that toppled Metternich’s system and forced princes and cabinets into provisional cooperation with liberal and national committees. The book follows the Assembly’s struggle to define the territorial scope of the future Germany—großdeutsch versus kleindeutsch (“greater-German” including Austria’s German lands versus “lesser-German” under Prussia without the Habsburg monarchy)—the catalogue of fundamental rights of the German people, and the executive power that might give a constitution force. Sybel stresses the Assembly’s lack of an army and treasury of its own: its authority depended on the goodwill of the larger states, above all Prussia and Austria, and on the residual prestige of the old federal institutions. Debates over the Reichsverweser (“Imperial Regent”) Erzherzog Johann (“Archduke John”), over the provisional central power, and over relations with the dynastic governments occupy the early chapters; Sybel portrays a body rich in legal and historical learning yet weak in the instruments of state.

The narrative then turns to the military and diplomatic environment in which the Assembly worked. Wars and interventions on the periphery—Italy, Hungary, Schleswig-Holstein—drew off Austrian and Prussian attention and periodically raised hopes that one or the other great power might lend the national constitution a sword. Sybel follows the Schleswig-Holstein entanglement as a test case: popular German sympathy for the duchies collided with European treaty law and with Prussian cabinet caution, producing armistices and protests that damaged the Assembly’s credit among nationalists without securing durable gains. Inside Germany, the failure of radical risings and the recovery of monarchical armies narrowed the space for a purely parliamentary solution. Party groups inside the Paulskirche (“St. Paul’s Church”)—moderate liberals, democrats, Catholics, particularists—receive attention as they form and reform majorities on rights, central power, and the Austrian question. By the time the Assembly approached the imperial constitution and the question of the hereditary emperor, Sybel argues, the decisive fact was already clear: only Prussia could furnish the power to make a German constitution real, and Prussia’s crown would not take that power on revolutionary title. Austrian recovery after the Italian campaigns and the suppression of the Hungarian rising stiffened Vienna’s refusal to be reduced to a non-German great power outside a Prussian-led empire.

Zweites Buch — Die Kaiserfrage (The Imperial Question)

The second book is devoted to the Kaiserfrage (“imperial question”)—whether and on what terms a German emperor should be created. Sybel reconstructs the constitutional draft that vested hereditary imperial dignity in the King of Prussia, the negotiations and party alignments inside the Assembly, and the famous deputation that offered the crown to Friedrich Wilhelm IV. (“Frederick William IV”) in Berlin. He treats the king’s refusal not as a mere personal quirk but as the consistent expression of a legitimist and federalist conscience: the crown of Charlemagne’s heirs, in the king’s view, could not be accepted from a popular assembly without the free consent of the princes and without a settlement that preserved the monarchical principle. Sybel recounts the king’s public answers and private language, the pressure of Prussian conservatives and of the court camarilla, and the disappointment of moderate liberals who had hoped Berlin would accept leadership even with reservations. Austrian statesmen, recovering authority, made clear that a kleindeutsch hereditary empire meant the political expulsion of the Habsburg monarchy from Germany—an outcome Vienna would fight by diplomacy and, if need be, by arms in alliance with particularist courts.

Around the refusal cluster the secondary states’ calculations. Bavaria, Württemberg, Saxony, and Hanover weighed particular interests, dynastic dignity, and fear of both Prussian centralization and democratic excess. Sybel details the crumbling of the Assembly’s majority, the withdrawal of Austrian and then other deputies, the rump’s move toward Stuttgart, and the extinction of the parliamentary experiment amid military restoration. The book closes the revolutionary chapter of national unity: the constitution of the Paulskirche remains a monument of German liberal statecraft, but without force behind it the imperial project collapses, leaving the field to cabinets. Sybel’s judgment is that the Assembly educated the nation in constitutional forms and national goals even as it failed as a governing body—an education later cabinets would inherit and redirect under different titles and with different instruments.

Drittes Buch — Die preußische Unionspolitik (The Prussian Union Policy)

With the Assembly gone, Sybel turns to Prussia’s attempt to organize Germany from above through the Unionspolitik (“Union policy”). The Dreikönigsbündnis (“Three Kings’ Alliance”) of Prussia, Saxony, and Hanover becomes the core of a projected Erfurter Union (“Erfurt Union”)—a narrower federation under Prussian leadership, with a constitution more conservative than Frankfurt’s yet still national in ambition. Sybel follows the drafting of the Union constitution, the invitation to other German governments, the elections and meeting of the Erfurt parliament, and the simultaneous Austrian effort to revive the old Bundestag (“Federal Diet”) at Frankfurt as the sole legitimate federal organ. He gives weight to Joseph Maria von Radowitz as the intellectual and diplomatic driver of the Union idea at the Prussian court, and to the tension between Radowitz’s national programme and the king’s reluctance to risk war with Austria and Russia.

The narrative emphasizes dualism. Fürst Schwarzenberg (“Prince Schwarzenberg”) in Vienna works to restore Austria’s federal primacy and to isolate Prussia; Berlin under Friedrich Wilhelm IV and his ministers oscillates between national leadership and fear of European war. Sybel uses diplomatic correspondence to show how the middle states hedged, how Russian influence weighed against a Prussian-led reorganization that might upset the post-1815 balance, and how public opinion in northern Germany remained more favorable to Union ideas than many cabinets. Military demonstrations, mobilization measures, and the Hessian constitutional crisis tighten the confrontation: the question is no longer only constitutional form but which great power will dominate the Confederation’s executive and army. Sybel tracks the Zollverein (“Customs Union”) as a quiet Prussian asset—economic cohesion in the north and centre that outlasted the political Union—without yet making commerce the sole key to unity. Erfurt’s parliamentary episode is treated with respect for its moderating constitutional work and with clear-eyed recognition that without Bavaria, Württemberg, and Austria, and without a king ready to fight, the Union remained a torso.

Viertes Buch — Der Verzicht auf die Union (Renunciation of the Union)

The fourth book carries the Union crisis to its end. Sybel narrates the progressive defection of allies, the legal and military pressure Austria and the restored Diet can bring to bear, and the Prussian court’s decision to avoid a general German war. The climax is the Olmützer Punktation of November 1850: Prussia agrees to demobilize and to abandon the Union experiment in favor of conferences that will discuss federal reform under conditions favorable to Austria. Sybel does not hide the contemporary sense of humiliation in Prussian national circles; he presents Olmütz as a necessary retreat given Prussia’s unpreparedness and isolation, yet also as the nadir from which a later, better-armed Prussia under different leadership would reverse the verdict. He follows the road to Olmütz through the autumn mobilizations, the role of Tsarist mediation, and the internal Prussian debate between those who preferred a “glorious war” and those who preferred survival of the monarchical state without a premature trial of strength. The Hessian affair and the competing claims to execute federal law supply the immediate fuse; the deeper cause is Prussia’s inability to convert Union paper into recognized hegemony.

The volume thus ends with the old Confederation temporarily restored, Austrian influence reasserted at Frankfurt, and the national question postponed—setting the stage for the long dualist contest that subsequent volumes will follow through the Crimean years, the New Era, Bismarck’s appointment, and the wars of unification. For Sybel, 1848–50 is not a closed failure but the first act of a foundation story whose decisive instruments will be army reform, diplomatic isolation of Austria, and wars whose outcomes the documents of the 1860s will illuminate as carefully as this volume illuminates the documents of the revolutionary biennium.

Significance for this wiki

Volume 1 is the documentary foundation for Sybel’s Prussian-national reading of 1848–50: Frankfurt’s failure, the Union’s promise and collapse, and Olmütz as the reverse of 1866. Use it for actors (Schwarzenberg, Friedrich Wilhelm IV, Radowitz, the Erfurt circle), for the institutional sequence National Assembly → Union → restored Bund, and for archive-based detail on Prussian cabinet policy. Pair with Treitschke’s earlier narrative volumes for Vormärz background and with later Sybel volumes for Schleswig-Holstein and 1866.
