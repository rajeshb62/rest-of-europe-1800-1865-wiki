Friedrich List — Das nationale System der Politischen Ökonomie

Friedrich List (1789–1846). The deposited text is the Waentig edition in the series Sammlung sozialwissenschaftlicher Meister (Jena: Gustav Fischer; second edition of that series text), OCR’d as Internet Archive item dasnationalesys00list. List’s own title-work is the 1841 Cotta statement: Das nationale System der politischen Oekonomie, first volume Der internationale Handel, die Handelspolitik und die deutsche Zollunion (Stuttgart und Tübingen). The OCR carries List’s continuous chapter numbering with original-edition page markers in parentheses and a modern editor’s front matter (Eheberg/Waentig-style introductory apparatus).

What the book is for

The Einleitung (“Introduction”) opens on the gap between theorists and practitioners over internationaler Handel (“international trade”) and Handelspolitik (“trade policy”). No other branch of political economy, List says, so directly touches nations’ prosperity, civilisation, independence, power, and duration. England’s contemporary Manufaktur- und Handelssuprematie (“manufacturing and commercial supremacy”) is described as a consistent system aiming to monopolise manufactures, great commerce, shipping, colonies, and the seas, and to reduce other nations to manufacture- and trade-dependence “wie die Hindus.” Russia’s turn to a Prohibitivsystem (“prohibitive system”) is cited as yielding national prosperity; North America’s wider opening to English goods after a protective rise is cited as yielding “Konvulsion und Ruin.” The science that cannot light the path of practice is useless; the fault of the theory–practice breach lies with both sides. Political economy for international trade must draw its lessons from experience and calculate measures for the needs of the present and the peculiar condition of each nation.

Book I — Die Geschichte (chs. 1–10)

Historical chapters treat, in order: the Italians; the Hansards; the Netherlanders; the English; Spaniards and Portuguese; the French; the Germans; the Russians; the North Americans; then Die Lehren der Geschichte (“The Lessons of History”).

The English path (Navigation, protective rise to industrial supremacy) and Colbert’s France recur as positive school examples of state commercial policy. Germany appears as a nation long bled by internal splintering and by neighbours’ commercial regimes. North America supplies the laboratory of protective reaction after free competition with England. The tenth chapter generalises those stories into maxims for policy: free trade with more advanced countries can lift a people quickly from barbarism through pastoral and early agricultural stages and into the first beginnings of manufactures and shipping; a complete Manufakturkraft (“manufacturing power”), large shipping, and great foreign trade are attained only with Einschreitung der Staatsgewalt (“intervention of state power”).

Book II — Die Theorie (chs. 11–27)

Political versus cosmopolitan economy (ch. 11)

Before Quesnay there was only administrative practice for one’s own nation. Quesnay first extended inquiry to the whole human race and titled his work as most advantageous au genre humain; he asks one to imagine the merchants of all nations as one Handelsrepublik (“commercial republic”). That is kosmopolitische Ökonomie — how the entire human race may prosper — as against politische Ökonomie, which teaches how a given nation, under given world conditions, reaches prosperity, civilisation, and power through agriculture, industry, and trade.

Adam Smith, List argues, likewise pursued the cosmopolitan idea of absolute world free trade. His title speaks of the wealth of nations as of all humanity; he treats systems of political economy mainly to show their nullity and to put world-economy in place of national economy. War appears only in passing; perpetual peace underlies the arguments. J. B. Say expressly requires imagining a Universalrepublik (“universal republic”) to make general free trade intelligible. List’s national system is the counter-science: the nation remains the unit of analysis.

Productive powers versus exchange values (ch. 12)

Title theme: Theorie der produktiven Kräfte and Theorie der Werte. Causes of wealth differ from wealth itself. An individual (or nation) rich in Tauschwerte but without power to create more value than it consumes grows poor; one poor in stock but strong in creating power grows rich. Die Kraft, Reichtümer zu schaffen, is infinitely more important than wealth itself — it secures possession, increase, and replacement of losses. Germany, repeatedly devastated, saved productive powers and recovered; Spain, with peace and rich soil and mines, lost productive power and sank. The American war of independence cost hundreds of millions yet strengthened productive power through national independence, after which wealth rose beyond prior levels. Smith saw labour and the productive power of labour in his introduction, but was so dominated by cosmopolitan free trade and by Teilung der Arbeit that he did not carry through the idea of productive power as List requires.

National division of operations and confederation of national productive forces (ch. 13)

Division of labour is incomplete if limited to operations inside a workshop. List extends it to the nationale Teilung der Geschäftsoperationen and to the Konföderation der Nationalproduktivkräfte — the joining of a nation’s forces across territory, sectors, and institutions. Political and commercial fragmentation taxes that confederation; national economic unity multiplies it.

Private economy and national economy; nationality (chs. 14–16)

What is rational for a private merchant is not always rational for the nation. Nationalität (“nationality”) is an economic fact: nations stand at unequal stages; policy that ignores nationality serves the already dominant manufacturing power. List distinguishes popular/state economy and political/Nationalökonomie (“national economy”) in successive chapters as he fences off Smith–Say individualism.

Manufakturkraft (chs. 17–25)

A run of chapters ties Manufakturkraft (“manufacturing power”) to personal, social, and political productive forces; to natural productive forces; to instrumental forces (material capitals); to the agricultural interest; to trade; to shipping, sea power, and colonisation; to instruments of circulation; to continuity and “work-continuation”; and to stimuli of production and consumption. Manufacturing is the lever that raises agriculture, skills, credit, and political power together. A pure Agrikulturstaat (“agricultural state”) remains dependent when it only exchanges raw produce for foreign manufactures.

Stages and the limited case for protection (esp. around nationality / ch. 15 material)

In economic development nations pass through: original wildness; pastoral condition; agricultural condition; agricultural-manufacturing condition; agricultural-manufacturing-commercial condition (Agrikulturmanufakturhandelsstand). Free trade with more advanced cities and lands best speeds the early climb. Restriction of trade to plant and protect one’s own manufacturing power is justified only for nations that already have the spiritual and material means to plant that power and reach the highest civilisation and power, yet are held back by a more advanced foreign manufacturing power — and only so long as domestic manufacturing is not yet strong enough to face foreign competition, thereafter only so far as needed to protect manufacturing “in its roots.” A protective system that shut out foreign competition entirely and at once would violate both cosmopolitan economy and the nation’s own well-understood interest. Early Schutzzölle must be moderate and rise only gradually with capital, skill, and enterprise; not every branch needs equal protection — the great branches (large fixed and circulating capital, machinery, technical knowledge, many workers, goods of general consumption) claim special care.

The customs house (chs. 26–27)

Die Douane als Hauptmittel zu Pflanzung und Beschützung der inneren Manufakturkraft — the tariff administration is the chief means to plant and protect internal manufacturing power. A following chapter confronts the Douane with the ruling school’s objections (monopoly, consumer loss, etc.). List treats the temporary sacrifice of exchange-value as the price of building productive power.

Book III — Die Systeme (chs. 28–32)

Italian national economists; the Industriesystem (which the school falsely calls the mercantile system); the physiocratic or agricultural system; the Tauschwertsystem (which the school falsely calls the industrial system) under Adam Smith; continuation on J. B. Say and his school. List rehabilitates the older industrial/statecraft system as misunderstood historical practice and attacks the dominant school as a theory of exchange-value that dissolves the nation.

Book IV — Die Politik (chs. 33–36)

Insular supremacy, continental powers, North America and France (ch. 33)

England’s island supremacy structures the options of continental states and of the United States; France and America appear as counterpart cases of policy under that pressure.

Insular supremacy and the German commercial union (ch. 34)

Germany’s last twenty years show what a great nation is without a capable commercial policy and what it can become with one. Germany was, in Franklin’s phrase on New Jersey, a barrel tapped by all its neighbours. England ruined much of German manufacturing, flooded Germany with woollens, cottons, and colonial goods, yet shut out German grain and timber (and at times wool). German sales to England once dwarfed England’s much-praised East Indian outlet, yet the German was not even allowed what the Hindu was allowed — to pay for manufactures with agricultural products. France sold wine, oil, silk, and fashion goods into Germany while cramping German outlets for cattle, grain, and linen. Against that double pressure List sets the deutsche Handelsunion — commercial union among Germans — as the instrument of national economic self-assertion.

Continental policy (ch. 35)

Wider continental alignment against exclusive insular manufacturing and trade supremacy; the German union sits inside a broader continental commercial problem.

Commercial policy of the German Zollverein (ch. 36)

If any nation is called to plant a national manufacturing power, it is the German — by rank in sciences and arts, literature and education, administration and useful institutions; by morality, religion, industry, thrift, persistence, inventive spirit; by size and quality of population; by extent and nature of territory; by advanced agriculture and physical, social, and spiritual resources. From a protective system suited to its condition Germany may expect fruits for internal manufactures, foreign trade and shipping, internal transport, agriculture, and the maintenance of independence. The chapter is the practical programme: Handelspolitik des deutschen Zollvereins — external protection and internal freer exchange as the school of national manufacturing power. Closing pages discuss extension of the union (coasts, neighbouring lands, relation to Austria) as commercial-political tasks continuous with the same national system.

Apparatus in the deposit

The OCR opens with series title, Waentig as editor, and a long German introduction on List’s life, the Handelsverein, agitation for the Zollverein, and the place of the 1841 system — editorial matter, not List’s 1841 chapters. List’s own Inhalts-Anzeige lists Einleitung + four books (History, Theory, Systems, Politics) through chapter 36 and a Nachtrag. Parenthetical numbers track pages of the “Ausgabe letzter Hand.”

Notes for the wiki

  • Core lemmas in List’s own vocabulary: nationale System, politische vs kosmopolitische Ökonomie, Produktivkräfte / produktive Kräfte, Tauschwerte, Manufakturkraft, Schutzzoll / Schutzsystem, Douane, Zollverein / deutsche Handelsunion, Agrikulturstaat vs manufacturing stages, Konföderation der Nationalproduktivkräfte.
  • “Infant industry” / Erziehungszoll is later shorthand; this deposit’s running terms are Schutzzoll, Schutzsystem, and planting/protection of Manufakturkraft for a limited historical phase.
  • Glosses: Produktivkräfte (“productive powers”); Manufakturkraft (“manufacturing power”); Schutzzoll (“protective tariff”); Zollverein (“customs union”); kosmopolitisch (“cosmopolitan”); Tauschwert (“exchange value”); Douane (“customs / tariff office”).
