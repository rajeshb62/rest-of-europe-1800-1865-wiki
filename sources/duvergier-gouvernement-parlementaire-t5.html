Duvergier de Hauranne — Histoire du gouvernement parlementaire en France, 1814-1848, Tome V

Ernest Duvergier de Hauranne (1798–1881) — Histoire du gouvernement parlementaire en France, 1814-1848, précédée d’une Introduction. — Tome cinquième. — Paris: Michel Lévy Frères, libraires-éditeurs, rue Vivienne, 2 bis, 1862. — 604 fichiers, 22,523 lignes (~1.25 Mo en brut nettoyé).

This fifth volume covers 1819–1820, the sharpest reversal of the Restoration’s middle years. It opens with the liberal triumph of the 1819 press laws and something approaching genuine press freedom under Decazes and de Serre; it closes with the assassination of the duc de Berry, the fall of Decazes, Richelieu’s return on Ultra terms, and the exceptional laws crowned by the loi du double vote (“law of the double vote”). Duvergier’s thesis is parliamentary: liberty of the press and electoral honesty cannot survive when court, diplomacy, and a dynastic murder push the Charte (“Charter”) toward exceptional rule.

Chapter XIX — The Press Laws (1819)

The chapter opens on the progress of the liberal spirit, religious liberty, and freedom of the press. First debates map the parties in the Chambre des députés (“Chamber of Deputies”). The dotation (“endowment”) of the duc de Richelieu, the right of petition, and the loi de l’année financière (“financial-year law”) meet vigorous opposition from the Right. De Serre delivers a brilliant speech; first cracks appear inside the ministry. Inaction follows the proposition Barthélemy (“Barthélemy proposition”) and public agitation. La Minerve and Le Conservateur set the journalistic poles of Left and Ultra Right.

In the Chambre des pairs (“Chamber of Peers”) the Barthélemy proposition is adopted and the financial-year law rejected; sixty new peers are created to secure the government majority, angering the Royalist party and fueling violent polemics. By-elections follow. In the Deputies La Bourdonnaie, Royer-Collard, Lainé, Sainte-Aulaire, and Villèle speak; de Serre’s energetic reply has a prodigious effect and becomes one of the volume’s set-pieces. The new press laws are presented; Courvoisier reports; heated debates proceed largely without the Right, which abstains in calculated hostility. Benjamin Constant speaks; de Serre admirably defends the main provisions. Responsibility of printers, outrages against public morality, immunity of deputies, the jury question, and proof in cases of defamation against public officials occupy successive sittings. Officials resist; Royer-Collard and de Serre answer; Bignon adds piquant observations. Cautionnement (“security deposit”) for newspapers is debated as the fiscal bridle on journalism; Constant speaks; de Serre remarks on the Convention. De Broglie reports in the Peers; stormy debates end with the suppression of censure (“censorship”) and adoption of the three laws — the high-water mark of Decazes’s liberal experiment.

Duvergier then surveys the attitude and organisation of the parties, old and new newspapers, the law of accounts, and loans, with Casimir Périer, Laffitte, Pasquier, and Delessert. A petition for the bannis (“exiles”) exposes ministerial uncertainty; de Serre’s memorable speech resonates abroad and is read in foreign cabinets as a sign of French liberal consolidation. Violent press commentaries mark a rupture between the Left and the ministry once the Left judges Decazes too timid. In the budget debate La Bourdonnaie attacks; de Serre and General Grenier reply; Marshal Gouvion-Saint-Cyr speaks. Bignon’s “secret” and a parliamentary incident follow. A new de Serre speech on the exiles and debate on the comité directeur (“steering committee”) bring Courvoisier, Corbière, Constant, Royer-Collard, and a skilful speech by Manuel. The revenue budget, the School of Law, Bavoux, and disorders at Grenoble prepare the first application of the press laws and the end of the session. The author’s art is to show how a session that seemed to entrench liberty already contained the personnel and press wars that would destroy it.

Chapter XX — New Crisis (1819)

Ministerial embarrassment deepens under hostility from diplomacy and the court and the violence of the newspapers. Rumours of a coup d’État (“coup d’état”) circulate in salons and chancelleries. A project of great electoral reform is discussed in the press as either remedy or provocation. Press trials continue even under the new laws. The Brothers of the Christian Doctrine and the Concordat occupy Portalis at Rome in negotiation with Cardinal Consalvi. A papal letter to the bishops, writings on the Concordat (including de Pradt), episcopal discontent, an assembly of bishops, and their letter to the pope anger Louis XVIII, who dislikes episcopal collective politics. Negotiations resume; a provisional arrangement with the court of Rome and the bishops’ adhesion close the ecclesiastical crisis for the moment without healing the mistrust between Gallican crown and ultramontane clergy.

Electoral preliminaries follow Royer-Collard’s resignation from positions that had linked the Doctrinaires to the ministry. Elections bring defeats for the ministry and the Right; the election of Grégoire — the former Conventionnel — produces a moral and political shock exploited by Ultras as proof that liberalism ends in regicide. Duvergier paints the state of Germany under the Carlsbad Decrees, the murder of Kotzebue, and reaction across the Confederation, so that French readers see Decazes’s experiment against a European background of police and press repression. In France the ministry is shaken; Decazes’s position weakens; court and diplomatic pressure mount. The volume’s political temperature rises toward the catastrophe of early 1820.

Chapter XXI — Assassination of the duc de Berry (1820)

The session opens amid press polemics and the Isère election; Lainé, Benjamin Constant, and others already fight over the meaning of Grégoire’s return when, on 13 February 1820, Louvel strikes the duc de Berry as he leaves the Opéra. Duvergier narrates the crime, the dying prince’s agony, the court’s grief, and the immediate political exploitation of the murder. The Ultra-Royalist press holds Decazes responsible for a system that supposedly armed assassins by weakening religion and authority. The ministry falters; exceptional measures are demanded as the price of any continued government. The duchesse de Berry’s courage is recorded; the later birth of the duc de Bordeaux (the enfant du miracle) is prepared in the surrounding narrative as the dynastic answer to the crime. Decazes’s fall becomes inevitable as Richelieu is recalled and Villèle’s friends prepare to exact the price of Royalist support. Duvergier insists that the murder did not create Ultra strength so much as remove the last barrier to using the chamber majority against the liberal experiment of 1819.

Chapter XXII — The Law of the Double Vote (1820)

Under the second Richelieu ministry the chambers confront exceptional legislation: suspension of individual liberty, a new press regime, and above all the loi du double vote (“law of the double vote”), which gave the wealthiest electors a second vote in departmental colleges and weighted representation toward landed property. Duvergier follows the presentation, the commission, and the stormy debates day by day. De Serre, architect of the 1819 press laws, now defends exception after Berry’s death — the central biographical arc of the volume and, for the author, a tragic illustration of how fear converts liberals into instruments of reaction. Royer-Collard, Benjamin Constant, General Foy (in a notable parliamentary début), Camille Jordan, Manuel, Pasquier, Lainé, and La Bourdonnaie fill the lists. The law passes amid liberal fury and Royalist triumph. Civil disturbances and the first conspiracies of the Restoration’s middle years close the volume’s drama. The double vote becomes, in Duvergier’s later volumes, the original sin of the electoral system that the July Revolution will have to overthrow.
