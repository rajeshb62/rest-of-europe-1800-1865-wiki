Delord — Histoire du second empire, Tome II

Taxile Delord (1815–1877) — Histoire du second empire (1848–1869). — Tome deuxième. — Paris: Germer Baillière, libraire-éditeur, rue de l’École-de-Médecine, 17, 1870. Printer: Imprimerie Émile Martinet, rue Mignon, 2. London / Madrid / New-York Baillière houses listed on the title-page.

Republican journalist and deputy (Siècle), Delord writes a contemporary opposition history of the Second Empire as a continuous Histoire, not memoir. The series band is 1848–1869. This volume carries the running title L’Empire (suite) and is the second tome of the set (tomes I and III+ are not yet in this raw corpus). Body text runs through printed p. 721 (Fin du deuxième volume); table of contents follows. The closing paragraph points forward to the next volume for the Empire’s search for force after Italian war and internal weakness.

Core chronological span is the authoritarian Empire from the aftermath of 2 December 1851 through the Italian war of 1859 and interior politics into early–mid 1860 (Thouvenel at Foreign Affairs, commercial treaty programme of 5 January, Lamoricière at Rome). Thematic chapters on parties, press, clergy, Academy, and proscrits cover 1848–1859 as backstory to the same years.

OCR from local Anna’s Archive scan set (OCLC 6266334; Baillière 1869–75). Cleaned raw keeps title-page through table; internal double-spaces collapsed; front blank library leaves dropped.

Chapters

Chapitre I — La loi de régence (1856)

Financial fever, Haussmann’s Paris, Imperial Prince’s baptism, senatus-consult on the regency.

Agiotage and sudden swings of the public funds after government, municipal, and railway borrowings; Emperor’s moralising letters to Ponsard and Oscar de Vallée; Bank of France discount at 6 per cent; poor cereal harvest; monetary and food crisis; rent crisis in rebuilt Paris. Haussmann takes the prefecture of the Seine (from July 1854 appointment narrative); floods and the Emperor’s tour of inundated departments; “Paris césarien”; foreign princes; baptism of the Prince imperial and the golden rose for the Empress. Political situation of the Senate; Moniteur warning; opposition to the carriage tax; presentation and lively discussion of the organic senatus-consult on the regency (8 July 1856); oath of regency and La Valette’s failed amendment to bind the regent to the Concordat and liberty of cults; unanimous adoption.

Chapitre II — Le parti républicain (1852–1857)

Repression after the coup, prisons and pontons, plots and attentats, moral state of the party.

2 December 1851 as a blow not only to liberty but to workers’ associations (Castors, fraternal societies, sociétaire butcheries and bakeries dissolved across the departments). Approximate scale of arrests and mixed commissions; departmental prisons, internments, transportation to Algeria, pontons (Canada, Duguesclin), Cayenne; impossibility of a complete statistical account. Deaths and funerals of Armand Marrast and Lamennais under police constraint. Conspiracies and scares: Reine-Blanche, Marseille infernal machine, Hippodrome and Opéra-Comique plots; Pianori’s attentat, trial, and execution; Bellemare; Guinard; Pérenches; Angers échauffourée; Marianne; further departmental arrests. Moral condition of the republican party under the Empire.

Chapitre III — La légitimité — L’orléanisme (c. 1850–1857)

Legitimist organisation around the comte de Chambord, Orleanist politics, fusion, and decline of pure royalism.

Wiesbaden manifesto; Chambord’s mandatories (Lévis, Des Cars, Pastoret, Saint-Priest, Berryer); majority rally to the Empire and defections (La Rochejacquelein, Mouchy, Pastoret). Government measures against legitimist correspondents; arrests and the Correspondants trial; Vincennes plot; Ligue fédérale; Jockey-Club and legitimacy; Chambord and the Crédit foncier; legitimists and Russia. Orleanism versus legitimacy; stathoudérat idea; Bulletin français trial at Brussels; decrees of 24 January at the Conseil d’État; Bocher trial; origins of fusion; Chambord–Nemours interview at Vienna; death of the last “Louis XVII”; Bonapartist pamphlets and Troplong against fusion.

Chapitre IV — Le journalisme (1851–1857)

Press law after the coup, authorised papers, government and opposition organs, clerical and light press.

Public opinion and the French press compared with the English; cheap press, advertising, Girardin’s reforms, Duveyrier’s régie générale des annonces, the roman-feuilleton. Press the day after the coup; new legislation; precautions against French and foreign papers; authorised list. Government papers: Mirès buys the Constitutionnel (Morny and Véron’s share at 1,200,000 francs); Arthur de La Guéronnière at Constitutionnel and Pays; Delamarre and La Patrie. Opposition: Havin and Le Siècle; Journal des Débats (Bertin, Saint-Marc Girardin, de Sacy); Assemblée nationale; Lourdoueix and the Gazette de France; Laurentie and L’Union; Veuillot and L’Univers. Girardin under the administrative regime; Villemessant’s Figaro; chronicle and clerical press.

Chapitre V — Le clergé (1848–1857)

Church and Empire, Sibour, classical studies quarrel, Concordat politics, Immaculate Conception.

Religious quarrels reborn with the Empire; July Monarchy and February Revolution vis-à-vis the Church; Falloux-era public instruction law; congregations working for the imperial foundation; Sibour as archbishop of Paris; alliance of philosophy and religion; fête des Écoles. Classical studies dispute between L’Univers and the archbishop; part of the episcopate for Veuillot; encyclical of 21 March 1853; defeat of the archbishop of Paris. Conditions the Pope attaches to any journey for Napoleon III’s sacre; surveillance of the pulpit; Lacordaire at Saint-Roch; liberal Catholics and ultramontanes; proclamation of the Immaculate Conception.

Chapitre VI — L’Académie — L’Université — La littérature (1850–1857)

Academic politics, Fortoul’s university reform, literary ralliés.

Opposition and parties inside the Academy; receptions of Montalembert, Musset, Dupanloup; moral prize split between Père Gratry and Jules Simon; Berryer’s refusal of the traditional visit to the Emperor (correspondence with Mocquard); secret sittings; Broglie; Laboremus; de Sacy; deaths of Molé; Falloux; Guizot’s influence; new class of the Academy of moral and political sciences by decree; reform decree. Salons. Fortoul as minister of public instruction; university reform; Dumas replaces Thiers as vice-president of the Conseil général de l’instruction publique; suppression of philosophy teaching in the lycées; bifurcation; death of Fortoul. Literature: few writers rally to the Empire — Sainte-Beuve, Mérimée, Théophile Gautier among those named.

Chapitre VII — 1857

Sibour’s murder, last legislature of 1852–57, elections, deaths of Béranger and Cavaignac.

Opening of 1857 marked by the assassination of Archbishop Sibour by the interdicted priest Verger; trial and execution. Last session of the 1852–57 legislature: free trade versus protection; 100,000-franc dotation to Marshal Pélissier; military code revision and rejection of attenuating circumstances; renewal of the Bank of France privilege (Devinck, Königswarter); transatlantic mail steamers; great works of Paris; tax on immovable property; Grand-Central; purchase of Longwood. Elections; death of Béranger; inauguration of the Louvre; death of General Cavaignac; opening of the new session, verification of powers, contested elections, Migeon affair; close of 1857.

Chapitre VIII — 1858 (Attentat d’Orsini)

14 January bombs, trial, letters to the Emperor, execution.

New Year diplomatic reception at the Tuileries; nuncio’s speech and the Emperor’s reply; general calm before the blow. Orsini’s antecedents and English sojourn; search for accomplices; bomb manufacture; Orsini and Pieri in Paris; police failures; date fixed for 14 January; Pieri’s arrest on the rue Le Peletier sidewalk does not alter ordinary precautions. Triple explosion; Emperor, Empress, General Roguet; the Emperor at the Opéra; mood of the boulevards; return to the Tuileries; arrests. Trial of Orsini and accomplices; Orsini’s letter to the Emperor; whether pardon was ever seriously considered; testament and last letter; execution.

Chapitre IX — 1858 (After Orsini)

Loi de sûreté générale, Espinasse, partial Paris elections, Montalembert trial.

Government attitude after the attentat; suppression of the Revue de Paris and the Spectateur; great military commands; Empress designated regent; privy council; colonels’ addresses; threats toward England; Milner-Gibson motion and Palmerston’s resignation. Discussion of the general security law in the Corps législatif; General Espinasse minister of the interior and general security; transportations of 1858. Partial Paris elections: Jules Favre, Ernest Picard, Perrot. Rest of the session: military exemption law, budget, nobility law, Paris works; renewal of conseils généraux; circular on charities that helps bring Espinasse down; Delangle succeeds; Prince Napoléon minister of Algeria and the colonies; Cherbourg visit; Montalembert trial; death of the duchess of Orléans.

Chapitre X — Commencement de la guerre d’Italie (1859)

Plombières to Magenta and the entry into Milan.

Moral situation of the Empire and the need to “do something”; hesitation between Poland and Italy; choice of the Italian war. Sardinia and Italy; Cavour summoned to Plombières; 1 January reception; marriage of Prince Napoléon to the princess of Savoy; opening speech of the legislative session; how Paris and France learn that war is likely; Corps législatif debate (Brame, Picard). Italian situation: duchies, Naples, muratism. Government efforts to soothe opinion (Moniteur note, Congress talk); Cavour’s alarms; military preparations; public opinion; Empress declared regent; Austrian ultimatum; Emperor’s departure for the army. First operations: Montebello, Palestro, Turbigo; battle of Magenta; French entry into Milan.

Chapitre XI — 1859 (Solferino, Villafranca, Zurich)

From the Mincio to the preliminaries of peace and the Italian settlement.

Austrian retreat behind the Mincio; field works at Melegnano; combat and taking of Melegnano; French uncertainty and Godard’s balloon ascent; the Austrian emperor takes command; unexpected meeting of the armies; village of Solferino; battle of Solferino. Interview of Villafranca; Cavour at headquarters and his resignation; Emperor’s return to Paris and speech to the great bodies of state; Treaty of Zurich; Italy and the papacy.

Chapitre XII — Les proscrits (1848–1859)

Exile communities and the amnesty of 13 August 1859.

Refugees of 1848–49; proscription in Belgium (rigour of the Belgian government, daily life); England 1848–59 (La Voix du Proscrit, le Proscrit); societies after 1851 (Commune révolutionnaire, la Révolution); Barthélemy–Cournet duel; expulsion from Jersey; proscrits in Switzerland, Italy, Spain. Partial amnesties; decree of amnesty of 13 August 1859; debate whether proscrits should accept; return of the exiles. Chapter frame: an Empire victorious in two campaigns and aiming at European autocracy cannot appear to tremble before a handful of republicans — hence the political necessity of amnesty.

Chapitre XIII — Politique intérieure (1859–1860)

After the Italian war: press amnesty, cabinets, commercial treaty programme, Rome, “faiblesse de l’Empire dictatorial.”

Addresses of the conseils généraux (22 August) congratulating a war they had disapproved; press amnesty; Moroccan expedition; creation of L’Opinion nationale; embarrassments of imperial policy in Italy; China expedition; Billault replaces the duke of Padoue at the interior; signature and uselessness of the Treaty of Zurich; close of 1859. Thouvenel succeeds Walewski at Foreign Affairs (4 January 1860); interior embarrassments; programme of 5 January; commercial treaty; legislative session and contested elections (de Ferrière, de Dalmas); Italian affairs debate; Emperor’s letter to the Pope; encyclical of 19 January; Lacordaire at the French Academy; Thouvenel dispatch; Siècle suit against the bishop of Orléans; Lamoricière commander of the papal army; white cockade in Rome. Closing meditation on the weakness of the dictatorial Empire: youth and the schools hostile to the regime of 2 December; Corps législatif impatient of a role limited to sanctioning ministerial texts; the antithesis of Italian liberty restored by a government that suppresses liberty in France; the exhausted Empire asks where it will find the force to prolong its existence — answer deferred to the following volume.
