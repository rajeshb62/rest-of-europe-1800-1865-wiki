Duvergier de Hauranne — Histoire du gouvernement parlementaire en France, 1814-1848, Tome X

Ernest Duvergier de Hauranne (1798–1881) — Histoire du gouvernement parlementaire en France, 1814-1848, précédée d’une Introduction. — Tome dixième. — Paris: Michel Lévy Frères, 1871. — Final volume of the series, from the Polignac ministry through the July Revolution.

This tenth volume covers 1829–1830. It opens with the Polignac cabinet confronting the chambers, follows the conflict of the bishops and Rome, the address of the 221, the dissolution and elections of 1830, the Four Ordinances of July, the Three Glorious Days, the fall of Charles X, and the advent of Louis-Philippe. Duvergier writes as a participant-observer of the parliamentary school: the Revolution of 1830 is for him the forced execution of the Charte (“Charter”) against a king who tried to empty it by article 14. The volume is both chronicle and civic lesson.

Chapter XLII — The Bishops and the Court of Rome (1828–1829)

Resistance of the bishops and their protest place the Martignac and early Polignac governments in embarrassment. M. Lassagni is sent to Rome; Cottu writes against the electoral law. The king’s journey in eastern France; Lassagni’s success; fury of Royalist journals; submission of the bishops. The Morea expedition and public impatience in the departments show liberal discontent with a foreign policy that seems glorious yet irrelevant to domestic liberty. A ministerial memoir to the king at first has no result. Newspaper polemics intensify; dismissals; trials of the Gazette de France and of Béranger. The conflict with the bishops ends; Netherlands affairs and the Belgian Catholic-liberal coalition; Don Miguel proclaimed in Portugal; Russian setbacks in the East; Greek affairs. Chateaubriand at Rome. Illness of de la Ferronnays; Polignac called to Paris by the king; he fails to find colleagues at first. Portalis interim at Foreign Affairs. Situation of parties on the eve of the session; convocation of the chambers; discussion of municipal and departmental laws; rumours. Duvergier uses the Roman and episcopal crisis to show that even the clergy cannot be governed by pure court will without negotiation — a lesson Charles X will not apply to the political chambers.

Chapter XLIII — The Session and the Address of the 221 (1830)

The session opens under Polignac. The king’s speech asserts royal prerogative; the Chambre des députés (“Chamber of Deputies”) answers with the famous address of the 221 deputies declaring that the ministry does not have the confidence of the nation. Royer-Collard, as president, embodies Doctrinaire gravity; Guizot, Thiers’s friends in the press, and the left liberals unite on the constitutional point: the king reigns but the ministry must answer to the chamber. Charles X receives the address coldly and dissolves. Duvergier analyses article 14 of the Charter — the disputed clause on ordinances for the security of the state — as the legal battlefield. Public meetings, electoral committees, and the association for refusal of taxes prepare the country for a struggle that is still, in liberal minds, legal and parliamentary rather than revolutionary.

Chapter XLIV — The Elections of 1830

Dissolution sends the country to the polls under the double vote and septennial rules still in force. The campaign is a referendum on Polignac. Liberal organisation in Paris and the provinces, the role of the National (Thiers, Mignet, Carrel), and Ultra efforts through prefects and clergy fill Duvergier’s pages. The result is a reinforced opposition: the 221 return stronger. The king and Polignac refuse the plain meaning of the vote. Military expeditions (Algiers) are used to distract opinion; Duvergier notes the capture of Algiers as a colonial success that cannot save a domestic coup. The court prepares extraordinary measures while still speaking the language of the Charter.

Chapter XLV — The Address and the Ordinances (July 1830)

After the elections the ministry reports to the king. Confidence of the ministers contrasts with the agitation of journalists. Meetings at Dupin’s and in the offices of the National prepare resistance. The Four Ordinances of 25 July 1830 suspend freedom of the press, dissolve the new chamber before it meets, alter the electoral law without the chambers, and convoke a rump electorate — a coup d’État (“coup d’état”) in the form of ordinances. Publication and the report to the king; journalists’ protest drafted in legal terms; printers and deputies hesitate then stiffen. Duvergier insists on the lawyers’ and journalists’ first response: not yet revolution, but refusal to obey illegal ordinances.

Chapter XLVI — The July Revolution (1830)

Street fighting of the Trois Glorieuses (“Three Glorious Days”) — 27, 28, 29 July — transforms legal resistance into insurrection. Barricades, the tricolour, defections in the line, the hesitation of Marmont, the poltroonery or panic of parts of the court, the courage of polytechnicians and workers fill the narrative. Charles X withdraws ordinances too late, then abdicates in favour of the child duc de Bordeaux with the duc d’Angoulême’s renunciation — a combination Paris will not accept. The Hôtel de Ville (“City Hall”), Lafayette, the duc d’Orléans as lieutenant-general, and the revision of the Charter into the Charter of 1830 close the Restoration. Duvergier’s final judgment is parliamentary: a king who dissolves the contract cannot appeal to its letter; the July monarchy inherits the task of making ministerial responsibility real. The ten-volume history ends where a new experiment in parliamentary government begins.
