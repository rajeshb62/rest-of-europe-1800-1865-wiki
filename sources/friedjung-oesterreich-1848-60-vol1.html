Friedjung — Österreich von 1848 bis 1860, Vol. I: Die Jahre der Revolution und der Reform 1848 bis 1851

Heinrich Friedjung (1851–1920) — Österreich von 1848 bis 1860. Erster Band: Die Jahre der Revolution und der Reform 1848 bis 1851. — Stuttgart and Berlin: J. G. Cotta’sche Buchhandlung, 4th edition, 1918 (first published 1908).

The first of two volumes of Friedjung’s classic narrative of Austria from the revolution of 1848 to the eve of the new era of 1860. The present volume carries the story from the collapse of the Metternich system through the March revolution in Vienna, the revolutionary movement in Hungary and the October revolution, the accession of Francis Joseph, the constitutional labors at Vienna, Frankfurt, and Kremsier, the winter campaign in Hungary, the subjugation of Hungary, the constitution of 4 March 1849, and the administrative, economic, and agrarian reforms, down to the revocation of the constitution on 31 December 1851 and the restoration of absolutism.

Friedjung’s account is a Liberal-national one, written with the materials of the Vienna and Hungarian archives and informed by his own experience of the nationalities question. The opening book interprets the Metternich system not as mere doctrine of legitimacy but as the practical defense of a polyglot monarchy against liberal and national dissolution; the narrative then follows the revolution as the collision between the imperial idea of a united Austria and the competing nationalisms, above all the Magyar. Throughout, Alexander Bach figures as a central, ambiguous personality — the revolutionary advocate who became the architect of the restored absolutism.

The volume is approximately 520 pages of text (plus a register), extracted from a Michigan State University scan (Anna’s Archive), 554 scanned pages. A detailed Inhalt des ersten Bandes opens the volume, and it closes with an appendix of eleven documents, including the protocol of the State Conference of 14 March 1848, rescripts of Emperor Ferdinand, Bach’s memorandum on the Hungarian-Croatian crisis, and the trial of Count Batthyány.

Content Summary

Einleitung — Introduction

Friedjung’s preface situating the work within the history of the monarchy and the nationality conflict.

Erstes Buch — Vor der Revolution (Before the Revolution)

The Metternich system as the result of Austria’s internal condition: the opposition of Archdukes Karl and Johann, Emperor Francis’s failure to settle the succession, the State Conference under Ferdinand, Metternich on Hungary, neglected reforms, the plans of Archduchess Sophie for the elevation of Archduke Francis Joseph, and the notion of summoning an imperial representative body.

Zweites Buch — Die Wiener Märzrevolution (The Vienna March Revolution)

The Austrian diets before 1848 and the liberal opposition in the Lower Austrian diet; the petition of the Viennese burghers of 6 March, the rising of 13 March, the resignation of Metternich, the granting of a constitution on 15 March, the Pillersdorf constitution of 25 April, the democratic rising of 15 May, the Wessenberg–Doblhoff ministry with Bach as justice minister, the abolition of the feudal Frohnden by the Austrian Reichstag, and the suppression of the rising of 14 September.

Drittes Buch — Ungarn und die Oktoberrevolution (Hungary and the October Revolution)

Count Széchenyi, Kossuth, Deák, and Count Batthyány; Kossuth’s speech of 3 March 1848; the Palatine Archduke Stephen’s part in securing a responsible Hungarian ministry; Batthyány named minister-president; the appointment of Jellačić as Ban of Croatia; the Hungarian April constitution of 1848; Kossuth’s finance speeches against Austria; Windischgrätz’s suppression of the Whitsun rising in Prague; the Serb rising against Hungary; the deposition and reinstatement of Jellačić; the Hungarian Reichstag’s war measures; the murder of General Lamberg at Pest; the rising in Vienna on 6 October and the murder of the war minister Latour; Windischgrätz’s march on Vienna and the reconquest of the city; the executions of Messenhauser and Robert Blum.

Viertes Buch — Thronbesteigung Kaiser Franz Josephs (The Accession of Emperor Francis Joseph)

The position of the army; the plans of Windischgrätz; the formation of the Schwarzenberg–Stadion ministry; the character of Schwarzenberg; Stadion and Bach as members; Archduchess Sophie and her relations with Metternich; the youth and education of Francis Joseph under his tutor Count Bombelles; the necessity of Ferdinand’s abdication; and the accession of Francis Joseph (2 December 1848).

Fünftes Buch — Verfassungsarbeit in Wien, Frankfurt, Kremsier. Winterfeldzug in Ungarn (Constitutional Labor in Vienna, Frankfurt, and Kremsier. The Winter Campaign in Hungary)

I. Die österreichische Frage in Frankfurt — the Austrian question at Frankfurt; Schmerling president of the imperial ministry; the German constitutional draft demanding personal union between Austria and Hungary; the proposals of Gagern. II. Der Winterfeldzug in Ungarn — the Hungarian diet declaring Ferdinand’s abdication invalid; the defection of imperial troops to the Hungarian cause; Windischgrätz’s invasion of Hungary; Arthur Görgei; the capture of Ofen-Pest; the concentration of Hungarian forces under Dembinski; the indecisive battle of Kápolna. III. Stadion, Schwarzenberg, Windischgrätz — Stadion’s constitutional drafts; the unity of the Empire; the free commune; Trade Minister Bruck; the ministerial deliberations on the future constitution; Windischgrätz against Stadion’s plans; Bach’s memorandum; the liberal-centralist and aristocratic conceptions of the state. IV. Die Kremsierer Verfassung — the idea of popular sovereignty; Bach in the Kremsier Reichstag; the relation of state to provinces and districts; the opposition of Palacký and Rieger; the adoption of the constitutional draft in committee; Stadion’s arrival at Kremsier and the dissolution of the Reichstag.

V. Die oktroyierte Verfassung — the imposed unitary constitution of 4 March 1849; unity of the customs territory; the strengthening of the monarchical power; partial recognition of the Hungarian constitution; the narrow civil liberties; the Grundentlastung (redemption of feudal burdens); Stadion’s illness and resignation.

Sechstes Buch — Die österreichische Frage im Frankfurter Parlament (The Austrian Question in the Frankfurt Parliament)

The revival of Austria’s strength; German particularism; the formation of the Gagern imperial ministry; Schmerling as Austria’s plenipotentiary at Frankfurt; the first reading of the German imperial constitution; the Austrian memoranda of 18 December 1848 and 17 January 1849; the victory of the Prussian ministry over the king; Schwarzenberg demanding the entry of the whole of Austria into the German Confederation; Schmerling’s resignation; the decline of the Greater German party, the election of the King of Prussia as German Emperor, and the refusal of the imperial crown.

Siebentes Buch — Unterwerfung Ungarns (The Subjugation of Hungary)

Windischgrätz’s inactivity; Görgei made Hungarian commander-in-chief; the second Hungarian advance on Pest; Görgei’s march to relieve Komorn; Windischgrätz relieved of command; the Austrians evacuate Hungary; the war in Transylvania and southern Hungary; the storming of Ofen; the deposition of the House of Habsburg by the Debrecen diet; the strength of the Hungarian army; the Russian intervention; Görgei’s capitulation at Világos; the execution of the thirteen Hungarian generals; the execution of Batthyány; the severity of the military judgments.

Achtes Buch — Militärdiktatur und Finanznot (Military Dictatorship and Financial Distress)

The position of the generals and the army in the state; the return of the court to Vienna; the reconstitution of the ministry with Bach as minister of the interior; the ruin of the currency; Finance Minister Philipp Krauß; the deficit and the rising war budget; the income tax; Kübeck’s influence; Baumgartner as trade and finance minister; the state of siege, executions, and police vexations; Hungary’s Honvéd officers in the imperial army; Austria in the public opinion of Europe.

Neuntes Buch — Die Verfassung vom 4. März 1849 und die Nationalitäten (The Constitution of 4 March 1849 and the Nationalities)

The repeated promise to execute the constitution; Bach’s circular of 15 August 1849; the ministry’s proposal of 29 December 1849 for the creation of provincial representative bodies; Stadion’s diet statutes and the curial franchise; the favor shown to the bourgeoisie; the Galician provincial ordinance under Statthalter Goluchowski; opinion in German Austria and the democratic party; party life in Bohemia, the Slovanská lípa (“Slavic Linden” — a Czech national society), the vacillation of the Czechs between historical and natural right, the conspiracy of May 1849, Havlíček and Palacký, the decline of Palacký’s party; the nationality policy of the government, the German state language before 1848, the principle of equal rights, and the Reichsidee (“imperial idea”).

Zehntes Buch — Reform der Staatsverwaltung (Reform of the State Administration)

Bruck and the reform of commercial policy; the creation of a unified customs territory and the transition from the prohibitive system to a moderate protective tariff; the plan of a customs union with the German Zollverein; railway building and the Semmering railway; reform of the political administration; the patrimonial jurisdiction before 1848; Schmerling and the reform of the judiciary; the creation of the Supreme Court; Bach and the administrative reform, the end of collegiate procedure, the district offices, and the communal law of 17 March 1849; the establishment of the gendarmerie; the educational reform under Leo Thun.

Elftes Buch — Sozialpolitik und Agrarreform (Social Policy and Agrarian Reform)

The national and social forces in Austria; the bureaucracy; the German bourgeoisie as carrier of the Reichsgedanke (“imperial idea”); the petty bourgeoisie and the working class in 1848; the end of the privileged position of the nobility; the successes of economic liberalism; agrarian conditions before 1848 and the agrarian reforms of Joseph II; the height of the Frohnden before 1848; the Grundentlastung; the law of 4 March 1849 and the redemption payments; the redemption of servitudes in Hungary; the closed farmsteads (geschlossene Bauernhöfe) of Austria; Windischgrätz and the nobility against the Grundentlastung; the crown for the liberation of the soil; the historical merit of the reformers.

Zwölftes Buch — Das Schicksal Ungarns. Das Provisorium (The Fate of Hungary. The Provisional Arrangement)

Opinion in Hungary during the revolutionary war; Windischgrätz’s conservative administration; the memorandum of Count Dessewffy; Geringer as imperial commissioner; the provisional organization of 17 October 1849; judicial reform in Hungary; the diminution of Hungary’s territory and the five military districts; the first Austrian officials in Hungary; the German officials; the old-conservative party and its address of 7 April 1850; Francis Deák and his circle, Baron Eötvös, Baron Kemény, and Deák’s silence; the position of the Magyars toward a united imperial diet.

Dreizehntes Buch — Zentralisation und Germanisation in Ungarn (Centralization and Germanization in Hungary)

Haynau as head of the administration of Hungary, his arbitrariness and dismissal; Geringer as statthalter; Hungary divided into five administrative districts; Archduke Albrecht as military and civil governor; Bach for full centralization in Hungary; Transylvania under General Wohlgemuth and the organization of 12 May 1851; the wishes of the Rumanians and the Saxons, the destruction of the Saxon Königsboden and of Saxon autonomy; southern Hungary, Jellačić’s pan-South-Slav wishes, the boundary between Croatia and the Banat, and the Swabians of southern Hungary; Croatia, the Military Frontier, and Jellačić’s work for Croatian autonomy; the trials of the Slavic spokesmen and the fate of the Rumanian leader Iancu; the imperial journey to Hungary in 1852; the struggle between the old conservatives and Bach.

Vierzehntes Buch — Widerruf der österreichischen Verfassung (The Revocation of the Austrian Constitution)

The dispute over the best constitution for the monarchy; the arguments for absolutism; Metternich in England and Belgium and his dissatisfaction with the ministry; Kübeck’s formation and views, his mission to Frankfurt, and his advocacy of absolutism; Schwarzenberg’s decision to abolish the “Mistverfassung” (the “muck constitution”); the creation of the Reichsrat (Staatsrat); police pressure and the mishandling of the press; Schmerling’s resignation; the administrative banishments, including Havlíček’s; Kübeck’s interviews with the emperor; Bach at the crossroads; the ministerial council of 17 August 1851; the decrees of 20 August 1851; the commission for the revision of the constitution; the coup d’état of Prince Napoleon of 2 December 1851; the imperial patent of 31 December 1851 revoking the constitution; and the social and political significance of the return to absolutism.

Anhang — Appendix

Eleven documents: the protocol of the State Conference of 14 March 1848; rescripts of Emperor Ferdinand of 17 and 18 March 1848; Alexander Bach’s “Punkte der Ausgleichung der ungarisch-kroatischen Wirren” (summer 1848); Bach to Baron Doblhoff (end of September and 7 October 1848); Wessenberg on Archduchess Sophie; H. v. Sybel on the policy of Prince Schwarzenberg; extracts from Schmerling’s reports from Frankfurt; extracts from Prokesch-Osten’s reports from Berlin; the trial of Count Batthyány; and Bach’s memorandum on the Austrian constitution (autumn 1851). The volume closes with a Register (index).

Note on the text: The OCR is German Fraktur with the original long-s (ſ). The full table of contents (Inhalt des ersten Bandes) appears at the opening of the volume. Place-names appear in their nineteenth-century German forms (Ofen for Buda, Pest, Kremsier for Kroměříž), consistent with the era and source.
