Grünwald — Az új Magyarország: Gróf Széchenyi István

Grünwald Béla (1839–1891) — Az új Magyarország: Gróf Széchenyi István (The New Hungary: Count István Széchenyi). — Budapest: Franklin-Társulat, 1890.

A psychobiographical study of Count István Széchenyi (1791–1860), the reformer widely called “the greatest Hungarian,” written as the second part of the author’s larger examination of modern Hungary. Grünwald explains in the preface that, while the outward course of Széchenyi’s public life was already known from the earlier works of Baron Zsigmond Kemény and Miksa Falk, his inner life could not previously be illuminated because the content of Széchenyi’s Diaries (Naplók) was still unpublished; now that the diaries — kept nearly every day for thirty-four years — had become accessible, they supplied the material, with the help of psychiatry, to reveal the interior man.

The work therefore combines a psychological portrait with a political assessment. It traces Széchenyi’s physical constitution and nervous disposition, his friendships and loves, his recurring despair and life-weariness, and the love of the Hungarian race that gave his life its purpose; it then weighs his place in the national reform movement against Kossuth and the opposition, and closes with an extended examination of his celebrated prophecy addressed to Kossuth and of the meaning of 1848 for the Hungarian nation.

The book is cast as a series of fifteen numbered essays. The text is approximately 550 pages, extracted from a University of California, Berkeley copy (Anna’s Archive), followed by an errata (Sajtóhibák) page.

Content Summary

I. — The opening essay: the epoch-making individual and the law that new eras are embodied in powerful personalities; the maxim that only the weak love themselves, while the strong carry whole nations in their hearts. II. — The great passion ruling Széchenyi’s life: his love of the Hungarian race, and the single thought of elevating the Hungarian nation and securing its future. III. — His physical constitution: healthy, vigorous and enduring in youth, a gifted rider, fencer and swimmer; his long life and the autopsy following his death in 1860. IV. — His sickly, sensitive nervous system, the source alike of his agonies and of his exalted states.

V. — A nature of action rather than contemplation; his mind formed for practical undertakings. VI. — His longing for noble friendship and the difficulty ordinary men had in understanding him. VII. — His capacity for friendship and love; Miklós Wesselényi as his first deep friend; the love he found only in his thirty-fourth year. VIII. — The insufficiency of private life; his sense of emptiness and the search for a great purpose.

IX. — The dark depths of his nature — life-weariness and despair; the sources of his anguish in religion, art, friendship and love; and the noblest passion, the love of nation, compelling him to devote his life to his people. X. — His high cultural, national and moral ideals; his horror at the condition of the old Hungary; the setting of national rebirth as the goal of his life. XI. — His dark view of the nation’s condition; how contemporaries came to regard him as a pessimist. XII. — The years of work in associations and societies before he began his publicistic career; the need to reach the whole nation through the press.

XIII. — The deep effect of his appearance on the nation; the exceptional, original personality and the ferment he aroused in men’s minds. XIV. — The great movement toward realizing national interests as the strict logical consequence of Széchenyi’s ideas; the end of stagnation and the awakening of public spirit. XV. — The conclusion: the celebrated prophecy addressed to Kossuth in the Politikai Programmtöredékek (“Political Programme Fragments”), and Grünwald’s judgment that it was not fulfilled — the social revolution it foretold never came, and the Hungarian nobility abolished its own privileges freely and peacefully. The essay then reconciles the two conflicting interpretations of 1848 and joins the glory of Széchenyi and Kossuth as one idea in two stages: “Széchenyi is the beginning of Kossuth, Kossuth the continuation of Széchenyi.” It ends by affirming that Széchenyi’s great achievement was a nation raised up from its decline and ennobled, and that he belonged to the strong who carry whole nations in their hearts.

Note on the text: The OCR is Hungarian, printed in Roman type, with diacritics partly simplified or dropped; it renders the long German-influenced orthography of the 1890 edition. Running headers repeat the uniform line “Grünwald: Új Magyarország” on the pages captured. The volume has no printed table of contents; the numbered essays (I–XV) serve as its divisions, and a brief errata page (Sajtóhibák) follows the conclusion.

What Grünwald supplies

Béla Grünwald’s work on the “new Hungary” and the Széchenyi problem is a Hungarian-language deposit for reform conservatism and the magnate-led modernisation programme before and beside 1848. In this wiki it balances Kossuth-centred revolutionary narratives with the older bridge-and-academy reform current.

Narrative use

Széchenyi’s programme. Economic improvement, river and transport works, social reform from above, cautious constitutionalism—set against both Vienna’s distrust and Pest radicalism.

Clash of methods. Where Kossuth mobilises county and press politics toward rupture, Széchenyi fears that rupture wrecks the reform capital already invested. Grünwald’s framing helps entity pages state that quarrel in period terms.

1848 threshold. How reform conservatism fractures when revolution and war demand choices Széchenyi’s gradualism cannot own.

Limit

Use for Hungarian reform debate texture; for military 1848–49 still prefer Friedjung/Springer and the Hungarian war pages. Glosses: keep HU names with English gloss on first use.
