Duvergier de Hauranne — Histoire du gouvernement parlementaire en France, 1814-1848, Tome II

Prosper Duvergier de Hauranne (1798–1881) — Histoire du gouvernement parlementaire en France, 1814-1848, précédée d’une Introduction. — Tome deuxième (“Second Volume”). — Paris: Michel Lévy Frères, rue Vivienne, 2 bis, 1857 (reproduction and translation reserved). — 631 files, 23,157 lines (~1.23 MB in the cleaned raw).

Duvergier de Hauranne, an Orléanist deputy and member of the Tiers Parti (“Third Party”) under the July Monarchy (académicien, 1871), writes a doctrinal history of the parliamentary regime in France centred on the constitutional practice of 1814–1848. This tome deuxième (“second volume”) covers the Première Restauration et les Cent-Jours (“the First Restoration and the Hundred Days,” 1814–1815) — exactly the same period as the main body of Tome I (2nd edition, 1870), but in the first edition (1857).

The volume is divided into eight chapters forming a continuous narrative sequence from the fall of the Empire (end of 1813) to the last intrigues of Ghent and Paris on the eve of Waterloo (June 1815). The material is identical to that of Chapters I–VIII of Tome I (2nd ed.), but without the retrospective Introduction (1789–1813) that occupies nearly half of the first volume.

Chapter I — End of the Empire — 1813-1814

Departure of the Emperor. State of public opinion. Old and new royalist committees. Dispositions of the foreign cabinets. The duc d’Angoulême and the duke of Wellington. Salon of M. de Talleyrand. Mission of M. de Vitrolles. Flight of the regency council. Entry of the foreign armies into Paris. Declaration of the allied sovereigns. First resolution of the Senate. Brochure of M. de Chateaubriand. Proclamation of the municipal council of Paris. Act of deposition. Abdication of Napoleon in favour of his son and mission of the marshals to the allied sovereigns. Negative reply of the emperor Alexander. Louis-Stanislas-Xavier called to the throne by the Senate. Addresses and congratulations. Definitive abdication of Napoleon and Treaty of Fontainebleau. Dissolution of the regency.

His entry into Paris. Intervention of Fouché between the comte d’Artois and the Senate. Intervention of the emperor Alexander. Declaration of 15 April. Ostensible ministry and secret ministry. Louis XVIII at Hartwell. Divers advice he receives there. Indecision. Voyage of the comte de Bruges to Hartwell. Louis XVIII in London and at Compiègne. He refuses to accept the Senate’s constitution. Declaration of Saint-Ouen. Entry of Louis XVIII into Paris. Formation of the ministry. Numerous brochures. Opinion of Benjamin Constant. School of M. de Maistre. Opinion of M. de Villèle. Diverse tendencies of the foreign sovereigns. Louis XVIII names the commission charged with drafting the Charter. Discussion and drafting of the Charter. Preamble. Royal sitting of 4 June. Speech of the king. Speech of Chancellor Dambray. Octroi (grant) and the nineteenth year of the reign. Address of the two Chambers.

Chapter III — The Government — 1814

State of minds and parties after the promulgation of the Charter. Difficulties inherent in the Restoration. Bad organisation of the government. M. de Talleyrand. The abbé de Montesquiou. M. Dambray. M. de Blacas. M. Beugnot. M. de Vitrolles. The high council. Improvidence and maladroitness of the government toward the army, the clergy, the owners of national lands, and the press. Le Censeur. Departure of M. de Talleyrand for Vienna. Replacement of M. Malouet by M. Beugnot, and of General Dupont by Marshal Soult.

Classification of parties in the Chambers. Statement of the financial situation of France, by the abbé de Montesquiou. Finance laws presented and defended by the baron Louis. Foundation of public credit. Vigorous opposition of the Right to the sale of the state forests. Beginning of financial speciality. Indirect contributions. Customs laws. Law on the unsold lands of the émigrés. Statement of grounds by M. Ferrand. Lively debate. M. Lainé. Adoption of the bill. Proposition of Marshal Macdonald. Project of reorganising the cour de cassation (Court of Cassation). Remarkable report of M. Flaugergues. Rejection of the ministerial project. Diverse propositions and petitions. Arrest and acquittal of General Excelmans. End of the session. Allocution of M. Lainé.

Chapter V — State of the parties — 1814-1815

Change in the opinion of France. Causes of this change. The old and the new regime face to face. Opinions. Interests. Self-loves. Numerous faults of the royalists, above all in the departments. Voyages of the princes. Bad effect of these voyages. Errors of Louis XVIII. Banquet of the Hôtel de Ville. Monument of Quiberon. Ennoblement of Georges Cadoudal. Expiatory ceremonies. Views of the pure royalists: M. de Bonald, M. de Maistre, M. Fiévée, the Quotidienne and the Journal royal. Views of the moderate royalists: Journal des Débats, Journal général. Views of the constitutionalists: Le Censeur, Benjamin Constant. Views of the conventionnels: Fouché and Carnot. Mémoire to the king. Political reflections of Chateaubriand. Views and progress of the Bonapartists. Nain jaune. Religious quarrels. Funeral of Mademoiselle Raucourt. Writings of MM. de Saint-Simon and de Montlosier. Apathy and indecision of the government.

Chapter VI — The twentieth of March — 1815

Congress of Vienna. Arbitrary partition of territories and populations. Discontent. Napoleon on Elba. Mission of M. Fleury de Chaboulon. Pact between the conventionnel party and the Bonapartist party. Mixed intrigues of Fouché. Lallemand plot. Blunders of the police. Landing of Napoleon. Confidence of the king and the princes. Proclamations and ordinances. Sentiment and attitude of the various parties. Napoleon at Lyon. Discouragement succeeds confidence. Attempt at rapprochement between royalists and constitutionalists. Negotiation with Fouché. Dismissal of Marshal Soult. Council of ministers and diverse projects. The Chambre des pairs and the Chambre des députés. Constitutional protests. Propositions in favour of the army, of the Légion d’honneur, etc. Royal sitting and oath of the comte d’Artois. Enthusiasm. Defection of Marshal Ney and approach of Napoleon. Confusion in Paris. Article of Benjamin Constant. Last act of the Chambre des députés. Departure of the king. Napoleon at the Tuileries. Ministry he constitutes. Review of 21 March. Vain attempts of the king and the princes. Napoleon master of France. Reflections.

Chapter VII — The Additional Act — 1815

State of public opinion the day after 20 March. Peace and liberty. Attempts at negotiation. Marie-Louise. The emperor Alexander. Debates of the English parliament. Deliberation of the council of state on the declaration of 15 March. Certainty of war. Constitutional question. Dissidence between Napoleon and his counsellors. Addresses of the high functionaries of the state. Liberalism of France. Brochures and newspapers. Le Censeur. Interview of Napoleon and Benjamin Constant. Drafting of the Additional Act. Confiscation. Maintenance of the constitutions and organic sénatus-consultes of the Empire. Preamble. Outburst of public opinion against the Additional Act. Retraction of the preamble and convocation of the Chambers. Lively polemic. Writings of M. Viennet, M. de Salvandy, Barrère, Benjamin Constant, M. de Sismondi. Old and new newspapers. Federations. Embarrassment of Napoleon. Addresses of the electoral colleges. Ceremony of the Champ de mai (Field of May).

Chapter VIII — Paris and Ghent — 1815

Irresolution and contradictions of Napoleon. Clubs. Café Montansier. Imperial court. Court and government of Louis XVIII at Ghent. Manifesto of 24 April and report of 12 May. Internal struggles. Party of the government and party of the court. Attitude and correspondence of the duc d’Orléans. Views of the sovereigns and the coalised cabinets. M. de Talleyrand. Impotent agitation at Ghent. Offers of service of Fouché accepted by Monsieur. Diverse intrigues of Fouché at Ghent, Paris, Vienna, London, and the Vendée. Mission of M. Fleury de Chaboulon. Explanation of Fouché’s conduct. Writings on the war and on the means of preventing it. Summary and state of the parties at the beginning of June 1815.

Notes

Series: Histoire du gouvernement parlementaire en France, 1814-1848, Tome II (first edition, Michel Lévy Frères, 1857). This volume covers 1814–1815 (First Restoration and Hundred Days) — exactly the same period as Chapters I–VIII of Tome I (2nd edition, 1870), but in an earlier edition. Relation to Tome I: Tome I (2nd ed., 1870) adds a long Introduction (1789–1813) that does not exist in this 1857 edition. The eight chapters of the main body are the same in both editions. A researcher interested in the text alone may use either; one who wants the retrospective Introduction must consult Tome I (2nd ed.). Editions: The complete first edition appeared in ten volumes at Michel Lévy Frères (1857–1871). A second edition of Tome I alone appeared in 1870 (Michel Lévy Frères). Duvergier, born in 1798, was deputy for Indre-et-Loire (1837–1846) and sat in the constitutional opposition (Tiers Parti) under Guizot. Placement: Rest of Europe 1800-1865 wiki, beside Tome I (2nd ed.) and the later volumes as they are extracted.

Corpus: This volume sits beside Delord (Histoire du second empire, Tomes I–VI) as a French source on the constitutional history of the nineteenth century. Where Delord writes as a republican under the Empire, Duvergier writes as an Orléanist under the Second Empire. Raw: duvergier-gouvernement-parlementaire-t2.txt.
