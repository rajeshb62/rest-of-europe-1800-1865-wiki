Kornilov — Course of Russian History in the 19th Century, Parts 1 and 2

A. A. Kornilov (1862–1925) — Курс истории России XIX века (Kurs istorii Rossii XIX veka, “Course of Russian History in the 19th Century”), Часть I и Часть II (Chast’ I i Chast’ II, “Parts I and II”). — Second edition. — Moscow: M. and S. Sabashnikov Publishers, 1918.

The course is based on lectures given by the author in 1910–1912 at the Higher Women’s Courses, the Shanyavsky People’s University, and the Moscow Commercial Institute. The first edition appeared in 1912–1914; the second edition is substantially revised. The author taught at Kazan University (1889–1892), then at the Petersburg Technological Institute and the Higher Women’s Courses; from 1909 at Moscow University.

The course consists of three parts. Part I (283 pp.) covers the general character of Russia toward the end of the eighteenth century and the reigns of Paul I and Alexander I. Part II (272 pp.) is devoted to the reign of Nicholas I and the first years of Alexander II (to 1866). Part III (the reactionary period 1866 – famine of 1891–92; lectures XXVII–XLI) was issued separately (2nd ed., 1918) and is placed in the Rest of Europe 1866–1919 wiki. In the preface to Part III (1914) the author still allowed for a fourth part (the last years of the nineteenth century and the opening of the twentieth down to 1904–1906), but it does not appear to have been published.

Character of the course. Kornilov belongs to the liberal-populist school of Russian historiography. The course is attentive to socio-economic processes, the peasant question, the history of social thought and enlightenment. The author strives for objectivity, yet with a clear sympathy for reforms and progressive currents. The exposition is lecture-style, with detailed discussion of sources and historical literature.

Part I — Lectures I–XIII

Lecture I. General character of the socio-political process of development of the Russian people and state down to the end of the eighteenth century

The struggle for territory and its socio-political results. Principal features of the new socio-political process of development of the Russian people and state as they appeared at the end of the eighteenth century.

Lecture II. The position of Russia at the end of the eighteenth century, in the reign of Catherine II

The frontiers of the state. Significance of Catherine’s territorial acquisitions. Routes of communication. Population. Racial composition. Estate-class composition of the population. Position of the various categories of peasants. Urban estates. The clergy. The nobility. The intelligentsia and the popular masses. Development of enlightenment and the origin of the Russian intelligentsia. The schism — раскол (raskol, “schism” of Old Believers). Position of state power and its organs. Finances.

Lecture III. The reign of Paul I

His place in history. Biographical data. General character of Paul’s governmental activity. The peasant question under Paul. Paul’s attitude toward the other estates. Society’s attitude toward Paul. The position of the finances and foreign policy. Results.

Lecture IV. The reign of Alexander I (Introduction)

Division of the reign into periods. Biographical data. Alexander’s upbringing. His marriage. His position under Catherine and in the reign of Paul. Works relating to the history of Alexander I before his accession.

Lecture V. The first years of Alexander I

Accession. Mood and degree of preparation. First collaborators and measures taken in the first three months. Work of the Unofficial Committee. Its composition. Plan of work. Discussion of political reform. The peasant question. Formation of the ministries and transformation of the Senate. Results of the Unofficial Committee’s work.

Lecture VI. Society and economy in 1801–1805

Mood of society at the beginning of the reign and the level of political views. Principal journals of 1802–1805. Position of the popular masses. The free cultivators law of 20 February 1803. Peasant reform in the Baltic provinces in 1804 and 1805. Population growth. Colonization of the southern provinces. The Jewish question. The government’s attitude toward sectarians. Russian finances and financial policy in 1801–1805. The question of state transformations in 1803. The government’s educational activity.

Lecture VII. The second period of Alexander’s reign (1805–1807)

Russia’s international position at the beginning of the nineteenth century. The break with Napoleon. Czartoryski’s plans and Alexander’s attitude toward the Poles in 1805. Unsuccessful outcome of the campaign of 1805. The war of 1806–1807. The crushing of Prussia. Extraordinary preparations for war with Napoleon in Russia. The winter campaign of 1807. The Peace of Tilsit. The alliance with Napoleon and the sharp discontent in Russia caused by the Peace of Tilsit and its consequences. Opposition mood in society.

Lecture VIII. Speransky’s reforms

Alexander’s decision to return to internal transformations in 1809. M. M. Speransky. Drafting of the plan of state transformation. Beginning of its realization: establishment of the State Council and transformation of the ministries. The decree on examinations for ranks and on court titles. Desperate condition of Russian finances in 1809–1810. Speransky’s financial plan. Karamzin’s memoir On Ancient and New Russia. Fall of Speransky. Position of the cause of public instruction. Opening of learned societies.

Lecture IX. The Patriotic War of 1812 and the Foreign Campaigns

Immediate causes of the war of 1812. The break with Napoleon. Relative strength of the belligerents and the plan of war. General course of military operations. Mood of the army and people in Russia. Napoleon’s position before Moscow and in Moscow. Expulsion of the enemy. Alexander’s triumph. Transfer of the war to Western Europe. The campaign of 1813–1814. The overthrow of Napoleon. The Congress of Vienna. Alexander’s plans regarding Poland and Prussia. Talleyrand’s intrigues and quarrels among the allies. Settlement of the Polish question. Alexander’s mystical mood and the idea of the Holy Alliance.

Lecture X. Russia after 1815

Alexander’s return to Russia in 1816. The Polish Constitution of 1815. Condition of affairs in Russia in 1812–1815. Calamities and material sacrifices of the population. Cost of the war and scale of devastation. Condition of Russian finances. Rise of the national spirit. Condition of industry and trade. Influence of officers returning from the war on society. Spread of enlightenment in the provinces. Society’s hopes of Alexander. His mood in 1816. Concern for the maintenance of the army. The idea of military settlements, their origin and realization. Arakcheev. His characterization. Course of business in the Committee of Ministers and the exposure of abuses in 1816. Arakcheev’s role.

Lecture XI. Internal policy 1816–1825 (part 1)

Role of the State Council during the wars of 1812–1815. Restoration of its importance in 1816 with the return of Admiral Mordvinov. Mordvinov’s activity and the financial measures of 1816–1820. The customs tariffs of 1816–1819 and their significance. Development of factory industry under Alexander I. The peasant question in 1816–1820. Emancipation of the Baltic peasants. Development of enlightenment after 1812. Role of the universities. Penetration of mystical currents. The Bible Society. The Ministry of Spiritual Affairs and Public Instruction. Prince A. N. Golitsyn and his associates: Sturdza, Magnitsky, and Runich. The wrecking of the universities. Journalism after 1815 and the position of the press. Alexander’s mood in 1818–1820. His speech at the Polish Sejm.

Lecture XII. Secret societies

Emergence of secret societies after the Napoleonic wars. The Union of Salvation. The statute. Pestel and Mikhail Muraviev. Opposition of Mikhail Muraviev and transformation of the Union of Salvation into the Union of Welfare. Its statute, organization, and activity under four “branches.” Outbreak of indignation against Alexander in 1817. The question of a republic in 1820. Influence of the “Semenovsky affair,” the second Polish Sejm, and the Neapolitan revolution. Closure of the Union of Welfare. The Southern Society. Pestel’s activity. The Vasilkov board. The Society of United Slavs. The Northern Society. Nikita Muraviev’s Constitution and Pestel’s Русская правда (Russkaya Pravda, “Russian Justice / Truth”).

Lecture XIII. The last years of Alexander I

The emperor’s mood and activity after 1820. Accord with Metternich. Role of the Holy Alliance. The Greek rising and Alexander’s attitude toward it. Internal policy in 1820–1825. Abandonment of transformations. Dominance of obscurantism. Photius and Golitsyn’s resignation. Attitude toward the Polish constitution. Position of the finances. The customs tariff of 1822 and the appointment of Kankrin as minister of finance. Results of the reign.

Part II — Lectures XIV–XXVI

Lecture XIV. The reign of Nicholas I — accession and 14 December 1825

Conditions under which Nicholas came to the throne. The question of the succession. Alexander’s unpublished manifesto on Constantine’s renunciation. Confusion and interregnum. Nicholas’s negotiations with Constantine. Nicholas’s accession. The rising of 14 December 1825 and its suppression. Personality of Nicholas. Biographical particulars. Investigation of the secret societies. Reckoning with the Decembrists. Influence of Karamzin and the programme of the reign inspired by him.

Lecture XV. The first period of the reign of Nicholas I (1825–1831)

Collaborators of Nicholas Pavlovich in the early years: Kochubey, Speransky, Kankrin, Benckendorff, Dibich, Paskevich. The Committee of 6 December 1826; its composition, task, and activity. The peasant question. Military settlements. Speransky’s codification work. Formation of the Second Section of His Majesty’s Own Chancery. Direction of the Ministry of Public Instruction. Attitude toward the Polish constitution. Foreign policy. War with Turkey 1828–1829. The Polish rising of 1830–1831.

Lecture XVI. The second period of the reign of Nicholas I (1831–1840s)

Conservative principles in foreign policy. The Eastern Question. The Münchengrätz meeting. Guiding principles in internal policy. Legislative work. Speransky’s activity in preparing and publishing the Свод законов (Svod zakonov, “Digest of Laws”). The peasant question. Condition of the population. Material factors that prepared the fall of serfdom. Secret committees. Work of Kankrin and Kiselev on the arrangement of state peasants. Establishment of the Ministry of State Domains. Kiselev’s work on the arrangement of serf peasants. The law of 1842 on obligated peasants. Inventories in the western provinces.

Lecture XVII. Industry and finances under Nicholas I

Development of industry. Fate of individual branches of manufacturing industry. Competition of Polish industry. Merchants’ complaints. Kankrin’s activity. His principles and policy. Protectionism and its influence. Reduction of the growth of military expenditure. The wars of 1827–1831. The liquor reform. The monetary reform of 1839–1843. Nicholas’s own role. Kankrin’s concern for the improvement of cultural conditions.

Lecture XVIII. Enlightenment and social thought under Nicholas I

The system of public instruction. Nicholas’s views. Uvarov’s ministry. His principles. The statutes of 28 December 1828. The university statute of 1835. Moscow University under Stroganov. Position and development of the intelligentsia. Significance of the catastrophe of 14 December. Two channels of ideas: French and German. Schellingianism in Russia. Mnemosyne. The Lyubomudry and the Moskovsky Vestnik. Polevoy’s Moskovsky Telegraf. Nadezhdin’s Teleskop. Chaadaev and the closing of the Teleskop. Idealists of the 1830s. Stankevich’s circle. Bakunin and Belinsky. Evolution of Belinsky. Otechestvennye Zapiski and Sovremennik. Moskvityanin and the system of “official nationality.” Slavophiles and Westernizers in the 1840s. Socialism and Left Hegelianism. The schism and sectarianism.

Lecture XIX. The year 1848 and the third period of Nicholas I

The revolutions of 1848 in Europe and their influence on Nicholas. The third period of the reign. Foreign policy. Manifesto of 14 March 1848. The Hungarian campaign. Internal policy. The peasant question. Measures against the press and the universities. Uvarov’s resignation. Prince Shirinsky-Shakhmatov. Position of the intelligentsia. History of the Petrashevtsy. Incidents with Samarin, Aksakov, Turgenev. Prohibition on Slavophiles appearing in print. The Kiev federalists. The Eastern (Crimean) War of 1853–1856. Inevitability of the crisis. Death of Nicholas. General conclusion on the reign.

Lecture XX. The reign of Alexander II — Beginning of the peasant reform

The Crimean War and its significance. Characterization of Alexander Nikolaevich. His upbringing and political views. Influence of the Crimean War. First steps of the reign. Mood of society in 1855–56. Conclusion of peace and the manifesto of 19 March 1856. Speech to the nobility in Moscow. Beginning of preparation of the peasant reform. Activity of Lanskoi and Levshin. Attitude of the nobility. Memoranda circulating from hand to hand. Formation of the Secret Committee. Ya. I. Rostovtsev. Petition of the Lithuanian nobles and the rescript of 20 November 1857 to Nazimov. The government’s programme.

Lecture XXI. Drafting of the peasant reform

Attitude of the nobility to the government programme. Difference of landowners’ interests in the black-earth agricultural and the northern industrial provinces. Attitude of the intelligentsia: articles of Chernyshevsky and Herzen; the banquet in Moscow. Address of the Nizhny Novgorod nobility. Opening and work of the provincial committees. Unkovsky and the Tver committee. The approved programme of work. Attitude of the press. Evolution of Rostovtsev’s views. Opening of the zemstvo section. N. A. Miliutin. Revision of the government programme. The Editorial Commissions. Composition and course of work. Rostovtsev’s programme. Deputies of the first and second invitation. Death of Rostovtsev. V. N. Panin.

Lecture XXII. The Statutes of 19 February 1861

Course of work in the Main Committee and the State Council. The struggle. Manifesto of 19 February 1861. Analysis of the Statutes of 19 February. Composition of the legislation and content of the individual Statutes. Significance of the reform in legal, administrative, and economic respects for the peasants, the nobility, and the state.

Lecture XXIII. Society and the press in the reform era

Influence of the drafting of the peasant reform on society and the press. Position of the press in 1855. Herzen’s exceptional position. His programme of 1855–1858. Role and significance of the Kolokol. Differentiation of currents in society and the press after 1858. Radicalization of the Sovremennik. The Russky Vestnik. Position of the Slavophiles. Social demands of 1859–61.

Lecture XXIV. The peasantry and the beginning of reaction

Attitude of the peasants to the reform of 19 February. Disturbances and disorders. The Bezdna catastrophe. Reaction in government circles in 1861. Valuev’s policy. First manifestations of nihilism. The Russkoe Slovo. Opposition mood of the nobility. The Tver affair of 1862. The constitutional movement. Mood of commercial-industrial circles.

Lecture XXV. Opposition, the Polish rising, and continuation of the reforms

Development of the opposition mood. Proclamations of 1861–62. Young Russia and the fires of 1862. Arrests and exile. Split between liberals and radicals. Herzen. The Polish movement. Policy of Marquis Wielopolski. The armed rising of 1863. Intervention of foreign powers. Outbreak of patriotism. Fall of the Kolokol. Triumph of Katkov. Continuation of the reforms: Tatarinov’s financial reforms, the excise reform. The university statute of 1863. Secondary schools, women’s education, elementary schools. The statute of 1864.

Lecture XXVI. The zemstvo and judicial reforms

The zemstvo reform, its connection with the peasant reform. Drafting and content. Attitude of the press and society. Conditions of the zemstvos’ work. The judicial reform — drafting, significance, content. First steps of the new courts. Legislation on the press. Vacillations of the government. Revision of the press laws. Temporary rules of 1865. Mood of the nobility in 1865. Address of the Moscow nobility. Komissarov’s attempt. End of the reform era and general conclusion.
