Delord — Histoire illustrée du second empire, Tome I

Taxile Delord (1815–1877) — Histoire illustrée du second empire. — Tome premier. — Nouvelle édition. — Paris: Librairie Germer Baillière et Cie, 108, boulevard Saint-Germain, 1880 (printer: Coulommiers, Typographie Paul Brodard). 121 gravures dans le texte.

Republican journalist and deputy (Siècle), Delord writes a contemporary opposition history of the Second Empire as a continuous narrative, not memoir. This is the illustrated edition of the same multi-tome work originally published by Baillière 1869–75 (see Tome II in this wiki). The present volume opens with an Introduction tracing the Bonapartist tradition from 1814, covers the Restoration and July Monarchy periods of Bonapartist conspiracy, the 1848 revolution, Louis-Napoléon’s election as President, the conflict with the Constituent and Legislative Assemblies, the coup d’état of 2 December 1851, the establishment of the Empire, and the early years of the reign through the Crimean War and Congress of Paris (1856). Body text runs approximately 596 printed pages; table of contents follows.

Core chronological span is 1814–1856, but the narrative weight falls on 1848–1856 — the February Revolution, the presidency, the coup, the consolidation of the authoritarian Empire, and the Crimean War. The introductory chapters on Bonapartism under the Restoration and July Monarchy supply the pre-history of the movement that carried Louis-Napoléon to power.

Chapters

Introduction — Le Bonapartisme

Chapitre I — Le bonapartisme et la Restauration (1814–1830)

The Bonaparte family after Waterloo, dispersion of the brothers, the duc de Reichstadt, Queen Hortense, beginnings of the alliance between Bonapartism and the republican party.

Treaty of Fontainebleau (11 April 1814); the family’s disbelief in the durability of the Restoration. Joseph in America, Lucien in Rome, Jérôme in Germany, Eugène de Beauharnais as duc de Leuchtenberg. The duc de Reichstadt. Mme Letizia at Rome. Napoleon’s sisters — Caroline, Elisa, Pauline. Queen Hortense: her disputes with her husband, her conduct after Waterloo. Beginnings of the alliance between Bonapartism and the republican party.

Chapitre II — Le bonapartisme et la monarchie de Juillet — La tentative de Strasbourg (1830–1836)

Bonapartism in 1830, the Bonaparte and Orléans families, the Strasbourg attempt.

Danger for the republican party of an alliance with the Bonapartists. The Chamber votes to maintain the 1816 legislation against the Bonapartes. Hortense and her sons in the Romagna insurrection. Death of the elder son. Hortense received by Louis-Philippe. Bonapartist demonstrations at the Vendôme column. Louis-Napoléon protests against being suspected of coveting the Belgian throne. Departure for Arenenberg. Publications of the prince. Fialin de Persigny. The Strasbourg attempt. Louis-Napoléon sent to America.

Chapitre III — Le bonapartisme et la monarchie de Juillet — Tentative de Boulogne (1836–1840)

The Boulogne landing, Ham imprisonment, escape.

Severe opinion of the Bonaparte family on the Strasbourg attempt. Prince Jérôme solicits return to France; Thiers transmits the refusal. Louis-Napoléon in Switzerland; the French government demands his expulsion. Arrival in England; publication of Idées napoléoniennes. Bonapartist propaganda of the prince de Crouy-Chanel. The club des culottes de peau and club des cotillons. Formation of Louis-Napoléon’s military household. Return of Napoleon I’s ashes. The Boulogne attempt. Trial before the Cour des pairs. Barbès’s attempt. Persigny boasts of having fired on an officer. Ham captivity. Louis-Napoléon as collaborator of republican newspapers. Literary celebrities of the epoch in contact with the prisoner. He writes to Louis-Philippe asking permission to see his sick father. Escape from Ham. Prince Jérôme writes again to Thiers and is allowed to reside in France for three months. Death of the ex-king of Holland.

Chapitre IV — Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte est-il fils du roi Louis?

The paternity question: Louis’s marriage to Hortense de Beauharnais, the hereditary senatus-consultum, the reign in Holland, the legal dispute after the Empire.

Louis marries Hortense against his will — Duroc had refused her despite the First Consul’s insistence. The senatus-consultum on imperial hereditary succession. Louis becomes king of Holland. Persistent incompatibility between Louis and his wife. Hortense leaves Holland. After the fall of the Empire, Louis sues his wife before the Seine tribunal and obtains custody of the elder son. Louis’s letter to the Pope regretting his elder son’s participation in the Romagna insurrection, and disavowing paternity of the second son. Timid and irresolute character of Louis-Napoléon. Striking resemblance between Morny and Louis-Napoléon. Letters from Louis showing intermittent paternal affection. Louis’s testament declaring Louis-Napoléon his son.

Chapitre V — Le bonapartisme et la Révolution de février (1848)

Louis Bonaparte’s arrival in Paris, his letter to the provisional government, Bonapartist propaganda, election to the Assembly.

The republican party at the beginning of 1848 hesitates to ally with the Bonapartists. Louis Bonaparte arrives in Paris; writes to the provisional government; departs for England. Bonapartist propaganda; the bonnets à poils demonstration. The first-hour Bonapartists. Louis-Napoléon’s candidacy; elected in four departments. Agitation and troubles over the nomination. Assembly debate on validation: speeches of Cavaignac, Jules Favre, Lamartine, Buchez, Viellard, Ledru-Rollin. Louis-Napoléon validated; he resigns as representative. Bonapartism emerges strengthened.

Chapitre VI — L’élection du Président de la République

National workshops, the June insurrection, Cavaignac’s executive power, the presidential election.

The national workshops; Trélat’s attitude as minister of public works; the government tries to force workers to leave Paris. Bonapartists foment revolt. Falloux as rapporteur. Tumult on the place du Panthéon. Dissolution of the national workshops pronounced. The June insurrection; character of street warfare; protracted struggle. General Cavaignac named head of the executive power. The National Assembly’s error in not reserving the right to elect its own president. Grévy demands suppression of the presidency. Lamartine’s speech. Thiers’s speech: he turns decisively against the Republic. Marshal Bugeaud, General Changarnier, Dufaure. Louis-Napoléon elected President. He swears fidelity to the Republic. Cavaignac and his government.

Chapitre VII — Le bonapartisme et l’Assemblée constituante

Formation of the 20 December cabinet, the 29 January crisis, the Roman question, the end of the Constituante.

Formation of the 20 December cabinet: Odilon-Barrot as chief, Léon Faucher, Tracy, Falloux, Bixio, Passy, Léon de Malleville, Drouyn de Lhuys, Boulay de la Meurthe as vice-president. Resignation of Malleville and Bixio. The 29 January crisis; Changarnier’s attitude. Suicide of the Constituent Assembly. The Roman question: political, military, and economic situation of the Roman Republic. Assembly’s opposition to the Roman expedition. Ledru-Rollin demands the impeachment of the President. Napoléon-Jérôme Bonaparte attacks the President’s policy. Last session of the Constituent Assembly.

Chapitre VIII — Le treize juin (1849)

The Legislative Assembly, the rue de Poitiers committee, the 13 June demonstration.

Legitimist, Bonapartist, Orleanist pamphlets. Composition of the new ministry under Odilon-Barrot. Emotion produced by the news from Rome. Numerous protests. Ledru-Rollin’s speech: he declares he will defend the Constitution even by arms. The 13 June journée — a mere scuffle. Paris placed under siege. Faults of Ledru-Rollin and his friends.

Chapitre IX — La révision de la Constitution (1849–1850)

Consequences of 13 June, the Ham speech, the 31 October message, the Falloux Law, the 31 May electoral law.

The Ham speech. The 31 October message. Letter to Edgar Ney. Dismissal of the Odilon-Barrot ministry. Rouher minister of justice; Fould minister of finance. Law on liberty of teaching — the singular alliance of Thiers and Montalembert. Le Verrier’s proposal to move the École Polytechnique to Meudon. Fear caused by partial Paris elections. The Eugène Sue candidacy. The 31 May law. The President travels through the departments. Message of 12 November.

Chapitre X — Conflits entre l’Assemblée et le Président de la République

Publication of Changarnier’s instructions, destitution of Changarnier, the transitory cabinet.

Publication of the instructions given by Changarnier to the Paris army chiefs. Thiers’s speech. The day of defiance against the ministry. Dismissal of General Changarnier. The men of action are lacking. Order of the day. Letter of Louis Bonaparte to Dupin. The transitory cabinet. The Right threatens the President with refusal of a salary increase. Montalembert’s speech. Letter of the comte de Chambord. Law on the National Guard. Suspension of Michelet’s course.

Chapitre XI — La révision de la Constitution (1851)

Five revision proposals, the 14 July debate, rejection, the Saint-Arnaud ministry.

Five revision proposals are before the Assembly. Opening of discussion on 14 July 1851: speeches of Baroche, Falloux, Cavaignac, Michel (de Bourges), Berryer, Dufaure, Odilon-Barrot. Rejection of the revision proposals. Imprudent and threatening attitude of the revolutionary party. Louis-Napoléon’s speech at the inauguration of the Halles. Assembly vacation. The coup d’état project abandoned. Recall of the 31 May law. Formation of a cabinet of devotion. General Leroy de Saint-Arnaud, minister of war. Reading of a presidential message on 4 November 1851 to the Assembly. Louis-Napoléon’s speech to French exhibitors at the London Exhibition. Dangerous illusions of the republican party.

Chapitre XII — La proposition des questeurs

The questors’ proposal to secure the Assembly against a coup, debate, rejection.

The questors’ proposition. The minister of war, interrogated, first sides with the Commission, then retracts. Uncertainty of part of the Assembly. Discussion of the proposition. Amendment of Broglie, Daru, etc. Colonel Charras supports the proposition. Michel de Bourges combats it. Hypocritical declaration of the minister of war. The proposition is rejected. The Montagnards. The socialists. The Right. The executive power profits from the Assembly’s errors.

Chapitre XIII — La conspiration

The Bonapartist conspiracy — Persigny and Changarnier, Fleury in Algeria, Saint-Arnaud’s hesitation.

The Bonapartist conspiracy. The President protests his respect for legality at every instant. The Strasbourg and Boulogne precedents. The Bonapartist programme. Persigny at Changarnier’s. Fleury in Algeria. The President’s speeches become veritable appeals for his re-election. Graduation of his attacks. Message of 4 January 1851. Did the Assembly wish to overthrow the President? Message of 4 November 1851. Louis Bonaparte calumniates the Assembly. Captation of the army. Mission of Commandant Fleury in Algeria. The Kabylie expedition resolved to create popularity for Saint-Arnaud. Canrobert’s hesitation. Change of the Paris troops. Espinasse tells Saint-Arnaud he is ready.

Chapitre XIV — La nuit du 1er au 2 décembre

The evening at the Élysée, the arrests of generals and deputies.

The evening of 1 December 1851 at the Élysée. Arrival of Vieyra. Morny at the Opéra-Comique. The conspirators meet at the Élysée. The Imprimerie nationale. The police commissioners summoned to the prefecture; they receive Maupas’s final instructions. Arrests of General Cavaignac, General Changarnier, General Lamoricière, General Bedeau, Colonel Charras, Thiers, Greppo, Roger (du Nord). Persigny at the École militaire. Espinasse marches on the Palais Bourbon. Lieutenant-Colonel Niol’s noble threat to Espinasse. Arrest of General Le Flo and Baze. Morny takes possession of the ministry of the interior. Maupas’s dispatch to Louis-Napoléon.

Chapitre XV — Le 2 décembre

The coup d’état proclaimed, the proclamations and decrees, the deputies’ resistance.

The Parisian population learns of the coup from reading the proclamations and decrees. Proclamation to the army. Action of the Catholic associations in favour of the coup. The people have no means of learning the situation following the suppression of the newspapers. Meeting of the representatives at Yvan’s and at Odilon-Barrot’s. The President declared deposed; the Haute Cour convened. Appeal to the conseils généraux. Weakness of President Dupin. Meeting at Daru’s. Arrest of representatives on the place de Bourgogne and at Crémieux’s. In the morning at the Élysée, first apprehensions. Louis Bonaparte shows himself to the population.

Chapitre XVI — La résistance légale

The deputies at the mairie of the Xe arrondissement, the proclamation of déchéance.

The deputies assemble at the mairie of the Xe arrondissement. They pronounce the déchéance of Louis-Napoléon. They exhort the soldiers to respect the Constitution and summon them to defend it. The soldiers declare they recognise only passive obedience to their chiefs. Berryer’s allocution to the crowd. Two police commissioners order the Assembly to disperse. Magnan orders the mairie to be occupied. The deputies arrested and led to the caserne du quai d’Orsay. The deputies in the prison van. The Assembly of the Xe arrondissement has done its duty. The Haute Cour obliged to convene itself. Efforts of several representatives to rouse the people. Attempts to organise resistance. Appeal to the people by Victor Hugo. Michel (de Bourges) at the Balcon du restaurant Bonvalet.

Chapitre XVII — La journée du 3 décembre

The night of 2–3 December, Victor Hugo’s appeal, the barricades, Baudin’s death.

The night of 2–3 December. Victor Hugo appeals to the people in the name of the Montagne. The wall of the rue Notre-Dame-de-Lorette. The Commission consultative. Convocation of the people in their comices. Formation of the new cabinet. The representatives refuse to be freed by force. The Montagnard representatives at the faubourg Saint-Antoine; they call the people to arms. A barricade is formed. Death of Baudin. New and useless appeal to arms. Rumours unfavourable to the coup. Second hearing of the Haute Cour; a police commissioner orders it to disperse. The Temple quarter becomes covered in barricades. The Parisian population begins to stir. Proclamation of Saint-Arnaud. Meeting at Marie’s. Meeting at Landrin’s. Colonel Rochefort’s trap. Charge of the lancers in the rues Taitbout and de la Chaussée-d’Antin. Anxiety of the Bonapartists.

Chapitre XVIII — Les fusillades du 4 décembre

The barricades of 4 December, the fusillades on the boulevards.

The barricades of 4 December. Maupas’s anxieties. Demonstration at the mairie of the IIe arrondissement. The barricade of the boulevard Poissonnière. Occupation of the boulevard by the troops. Fusillades on the boulevards. Invasion of the maison Frascati. The first victims of the day. Irruption of the troops into the Cercle du Commerce. The barricades of the porte Saint-Denis. The battery of the boulevard Bonne-Nouvelle opens fire. Fusillade from the faubourg Poissonnière to the Chaussée-d’Antin. Acts of savagery by the soldiers. Fusillades on the Left Bank.

Chapitre XIX — La nuit du 4 au 5 décembre

Night executions, arbitrary arrests, Delahode the informer.

General terror of the Parisians. Lirieux is handed over to the gendarmes who prepare to shoot him; saved by a fortunate accident. Nocturnal executions. Cruel treatment of alleged insurgents. Arrests at the Divan de la rue Lepelletier. The informer Delahode designates the journalists to be arrested. Nocturnal feasts of the soldiers on the boulevards. Secret executions.

Chapitre XX — L’insurrection dans les départements

Secret societies, the Var, Basses-Alpes, Vaucluse, Bédarieux, Marmande, Clamecy.

Secret societies in the departments. Marseille does not rise at the news of the coup. Armed uprisings in the Var department. Summary executions. Martin Bidouré shot twice. Executions at Lorgues. The Basses-Alpes department. Uprisings in the Apt arrondissement, Vaucluse. The canton of Crest. Resistance in the Hérault department. The Bédarieux affair. Resistance at Marmande. Resistance in the East and the Centre. The Nièvre department. Clamecy.

Chapitre XXI — La proscription

The 5 December decree, military commissions, state of siege, deportation, the pontons.

Decree of 5 December 1851. Proclamation of 8 December and its contradiction. Formation of military commissions. State of siege declared in thirty-two departments. Arrests and expulsions. Prisoners packed into casemates; assimilated to common thieves. Prévôtale procedure. The three categories. Arbitrary power confounds all authorities. The highways fill with chains. Ill-treatment of prisoners. Decree of 7 January 1852. Representatives deported, expelled, or provisionally removed. Departure of the first convoy on 9 January. Prisoners aboard the Canada; their sufferings. Horrible diseases on board. Situation of the republican party. Arrests on the most frivolous pretexts. The mixed commissions. Prohibition of associations. The proscribed in Switzerland, Belgium, Italy, Spain, America.

Chapitre XXII — Les décrets du 22 janvier

The plebiscite, the eagles restored, the Constitution promulgated, the Orléans property decree.

Absence of liberty during the vote on the plebiscite. Result of the vote. Baroche’s allocution. The Prince-President’s reply. The archbishop of Paris addresses a few words. Restoration of the eagles on the flags. Promulgation of the Constitution. Organic law of the press. The Empire re-established indirectly. Emotion caused by the 22 January decrees. Employment of the revenues of the Orléans family property. Dupin resigns as procureur général. Montalembert withdraws from the Commission consultative. Gasparin and Rambuteau refuse to enter the Senate. The administration of the domaines takes possession of Neuilly and Monceaux by force. The question brought before the Conseil d’État. Baroche hampers the freedom of this body; the government wins by a single vote. Punishment of those who voted against. End of the dictatorial period.

Chapitre XXIII — Ave Caesar

Distribution of the eagles (10 May 1852), the Bordeaux speech (“L’Empire, c’est la paix”), the plebiscite of 21 November, the proclamation of the Empire.

Distribution of the eagles to the army, 10 May 1852. Allocution of the Prince-President. 7 July 1852: elections of municipal and general councillors. 17 July: inauguration of the Strasbourg railway. The Prince departs for the Midi. 9 October: the Bordeaux speech: “L’Empire, c’est la paix.” The Prince’s return to Paris. Royal reception; Louis Bonaparte and Morny embrace before the crowd. The curé of the Madeleine awaits at the church door. Salute to the Emperor. The re-establishment of the Empire decided. Convocation of the Senate. Plebiscite of 21 November. Gathering of the great corps of state at the château de Saint-Cloud. Billault’s speech. The Prince-President’s reply: the Republic alone can save liberty. Faults of the republican party.

L’Empire

Chapitre I — L’Empire et l’Europe

Proclamation of the Empire in Paris, the imperial family, the new right, the Eastern question, the Holy Places quarrel.

The Emperor at the Tuileries. Proclamation of the Empire at Saint-Cloud. Proclamation of the Empire at the Hôtel-de-Ville. The imperial family. The Empire before Europe. The new right. Hostility of the English press against the Emperor. Hostility of Emperor Nicholas. The question of the Holy Places. French devotion and Russian devotion. The Tsar decides to recognise Napoleon III. Dispositions of Austria and Prussia. Persigny’s mission to Prussia. Saxe and Bavaria.

Chapitre II — Le mariage de l’Empereur

The Montijo marriage, the civil and religious ceremonies, the Empress’s education and role.

Prince Jérôme advises his nephew to marry. The royal marriage and the national marriage. Mlle de Montijo; she is the queen of the Presidency fetes. Le Moniteur announces the Emperor’s marriage to Mlle de Montijo. Nuptial manifesto: representing a new principle, he must conserve that character in marrying. The father of Mlle de Montijo. Signature of the contract in the salle des Maréchaux. Civil marriage ceremony. Religious marriage at Notre-Dame. Presentation at the Tuileries. The Imperatrice’s difficult task. The 1 January reception. The official press and La Guéronnière. Portrait of the Empress by La Guéronnière; she is poorly prepared by her education.

Chapitre III — Commencement de la guerre de Crimée

The Eastern question, Menshikov’s mission, the Sinope battle, the Franco-English alliance.

Nesselrode’s memorandum. Nicholas’s overtures to Sir Hamilton Seymour eight years after. The sick man. Russia refuses to come to an understanding with France. Menshikov’s mission; he modifies his demands. Aali-Pasha and Fuad-Pasha. Dispositions of the powers. Passage of the Pruth by the Russians on 22 May 1853. Vienna conference. Nicholas I and Franz-Joseph. Austria separates from Russia. Orlov’s mission to Vienna: he asks for Austrian neutrality; Austrian refusal. The king of Prussia and his dispositions. The Russian fleet leaves Sebastopol. Battle of Sinope. Dispatch of English troops to Malta. Signature of the Franco-English treaty on 10 April 1854. Allied landing at the Piraeus on 25 May 1854. The programme of the four guarantees. Operations in the Baltic. Taking of Bomarsund.

Chapitre IV — La bataille de l’Alma

Lord Raglan and Maréchal Saint-Arnaud, the landing at Gallipoli, the siege of Sebastopol resolved, the Alma.

First troop deployment. Disembarkation point fixed at Gallipoli. The expeditionary chiefs have no plan. First appearance of cholera. Inaction and impatience of the army. The Dobroutscha expedition forced to retreat. Lack of remedies and transport for the sick. Disastrous retreat to Varna. Recrudescence of cholera. The expedition loses more than half its effectives. Bombardment of Odessa. The siege of Sebastopol resolved. Proclamation to the army. Council of war held on board La Ville de Paris. Occupation of Eupatoria. Saint-Arnaud wants to attack Fort Constantine. Inaction of Prince Menshikov. Menshikov takes a defensive position on the heights of the Alma. March and battle plan of the allies. Slowness of the English delays their first movement. Bosquet’s turning movement. The French appear on the Russian left. Complete victory for the allies. News of the taking of Sebastopol spreads to Constantinople and Paris. General disappointment. Death of Maréchal Saint-Arnaud.

Chapitre V — Balaklava et Inkermann

Allied positions, the battles, the Crimean winter.

Positions of the allies after the Alma. General Canrobert. March of the allies on Sebastopol. Battle of Balaklava. Battle of Inkermann. General Gortschakoff succeeds Menshikov as Russian commander-in-chief. Recrudescence of cholera at the start of winter. Appearance of scurvy.

Chapitre VI — Fin de la guerre de Crimée

The Piedmontese contingent, the death of Nicholas I, the Vienna conferences, the fall of Sebastopol.

A Piedmontese army corps joins the allies in the Crimea. Death of Nicholas I. The Vienna conferences. Fault committed by Drouyn de Lhuys; he is replaced by Walewski at the foreign ministry. Slowness of the Sebastopol siege. Public impatience. General Pélissier replaces Canrobert as commander-in-chief. Failure of the first assault. Combat of the Tchernaïa. Taking of Sebastopol. Canrobert’s mission to Sweden. Alexander II’s proclamation to his army. Napoleon III’s speech at the closing of the Exposition. The king of Sardinia and Cavour at Paris. Acceptance of the four guarantees by Russia. The unpeaceable dispositions of England. Menacing language of the English press. Panic on the Bourse. The brochure on the Congress. Austrian propositions to Russia. Russian counter-propositions. Isolation of this power. Alexander II decides to make peace.

Chapitre VII — Le Congrès de Paris

The Congress sits at the Quai d’Orsay, the Italian question raised, the Empire at its apogee.

The Congress meets in Paris on 21 February 1856 under the presidency of Walewski. It sits at the Hôtel du ministère des affaires étrangères. Etiquette difficulties. Prussia admitted to the Congress. Walewski’s propositions. Reply of the plenipotentiaries. Le Constitutionnel declares there will be no prorogation of the armistice. Note of the Moniteur on industrial affairs. Session of 8 April. Walewski calls the Congress’s attention to the situation of Italy. Napoleon III receives the plenipotentiaries at the Tuileries. The Empire is at its apogee.

Notes

Series: Histoire illustrée du second empire by Taxile Delord; this is the Tome premier of the illustrated edition (Baillière [1880–83]), corresponding to an expanded and illustrated version of the first volume of the original 1869–75 Baillière set (OCLC 6266334). Tome II of the original edition is in this wiki; the illustrated Tomes III–IV and original Tome V exist elsewhere in the corpus. Placement: Rest of Europe 1800–1865 wiki — the volume’s core is 1848–1856 (Second Republic, coup, early Empire, Crimean War). The later Empire (1859 Italian war, 1860s liberalisation) is handled by Tome II and later volumes. Voice: Contemporary republican narrative history; represent what Delord states, without modern scholarly reframing. Illustrations: 121 wood engravings in the text (not preserved in this raw OCR).
